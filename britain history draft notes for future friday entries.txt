The Compact of Tythew

A country with one trade needs one fleet, and a fleet needs one flag, one court of appeal for its contracts, and one person able to sign for all of it.

What was agreed at Tythew [St Davids] on David's Day 1424 was not a conquest and not a marriage. It was a compact: the tallaths surrendered their foreign policy, their navies and their coinage to a single crown, and kept everything else. They kept their own courts, their own levies, their own taxes and their own languages. The crown they created had no power to tax them directly and has never acquired one.

This is why a Kernish judgment cannot be appealed in Cartaw; why Sodrick keeps a court of four judges for a population you could fit in one Nadacow parish; why the Devon customs schedule is a separate document from the British one; and why, when a Mendevan asks who governs Britain, a Briton will usually answer with a question about what you mean by govern.

Cadwallan II, who took the crown, is not much remembered, and the anniversary that matters here is the Compact's rather than his. That it was sworn on David's Day was not an accident either — the tallaths chose the one day of the year on which every one of them was already holding a feast. The consequence is that the New Year, the patron's day and the founding of the kingdom are the same festival, which is a large part of why it is the way it is.

The war they left early

The Wars of Fealty began over the Redstone Chair, a throne Britain had no claim to and no use for. Britain nevertheless entered from strength and spent the first years extracting concessions from Markland, culminating in the victory at Rexam in 1483, at which the last Duke of Chester was killed. The attempt on the city of Chester shortly afterward was rebuffed, and at that point — with the border where it wanted it — Britain simply stopped.

The rest of Albion fought for another thirty years. Britons regard this as statecraft and everyone else on the island regards it as the national character; both parties are correct. When the Second Great Dying arrived in 1518 it found Markland and English Kent exhausted and Britain not, and the two of them buried something like twice as many people per head as their neighbours across the Rustigh. Nothing this kingdom has ever done paid so well as leaving a war early.

The marriages of the period repay attention, and are easy to miss because none of them are British on both sides. Margaret of Iscombe [Exeter] married Mark of Tolose, and her children were Ambrose the Ascendant of Vascony and his elder sister Alexandra — so the house that made the Novomundane Landfall had a Devoner mother. Alexandra's own second marriage, contracted during the Wars of Fealty and before her famous voyage, was into Wales. Dormaget's film of 1934 is clearer about this than most of the histories, being built on cuts between the Vascon court at Huzatre [Bayonne] and the Welsh one at Cartaw.

Howel's road

Howel spent the peace after 1483 on harbours. By the first decade of the sixteenth century there was a chain of British victualling stations down the Atlantic coast of Africa — not colonies, and not intended as colonies, but a road. The object was to reach Asia directly by sea and to stop paying the Middlesea for the privilege of buying pepper.

In 1516 the Phœbos under Martan mac Kellot went down that road and kept going: round Africa, into Ungotia [Zanzibar], then handed on by Omani and afterwards Chinese pilots through Gurjat and Madray, and at last to Borune [Borneo], where the Morrack ship Cynthia was already at anchor. The Great Meeting of 1517 followed.

The story that the two crews could not understand one another is a fabrication of the nineteenth century. A dozen men aboard the Phœbos had Arabic and two could write it, the British and Morrack trade meshes having overlapped in Gaul and Spain for two hundred years. What actually passed between the ships is unknown, because the four pages of al-Casmi's otherwise immaculate log covering that week are missing. In the 1927 film mac Kellot's first mate hails the Cynthia with O Gastor 'ila Bolux, "from Castor to Pollux". There is no evidence for this and everyone prefers it anyway.

mac Kellot declined to continue around the world, having by then been told how much ocean was left. Four years later the Hangthen Edict named the Morrack and British ships by way of explaining why the Southern Empire would be tying its own ropes around the globe.

The Gulf, and the treaty

In the seventeenth century British ships founded staddomains along the Mendevan Gulf, and those staddomains are us.

You should read the next part slowly, because your schoolbooks may have been gentle with it. The Becouin Treaty of 1621 set out how Santrafew was to deal with the Mendevan nations around it, and it held for two generations. It ended after the British sack of that city, in which the Mastoisèt were said — conveniently, and on no evidence that has survived — to have collaborated. The treaty died with the accusation, and the settlers who followed took its death as a licence.

That is the founding act of British Mendeva. Britain has never much cared to discuss it. Until recently, neither did we.

The People's King

The Democratic Wars of the mid-eighteenth century reached everywhere: Britain and China were quarrelling over Madray at one end of the world while London made itself a free city at the other. What came out of them here was not a republic and not a restoration.

The tallaths did the only thing their constitution had ever equipped them to do, which was to swear the Compact again. In 1757 they swore it at Tythew, on David's Day, as in 1424 — and this time they granted the crown to a man who had no hereditary claim worth the name. Owain V is remembered as the People's King, and the terms he accepted are the terms his successors still hold the throne on.

Britain is therefore a monarchy whose monarch reigns on a contract that is re-read aloud every March. This is not the same as Borland, which dissolved its monarchy in 1894 and considered the matter closed, and it is not the same as London, which kept a queen and gave her nothing to do. The bicentenary of Owain's coronation was celebrated at Deganny within living memory and was, by every account, indistinguishable from an ordinary New Year except in duration.

The century of the crossing

The nineteenth century is the awkward one, and you are the awkwardness.

Britain led the Workshop period, and the Merch histories concede it in so many words: nobody matched the globe-spanning workshop and household vanguard of the British. Killdark was got out of the ground at Killdara in the Irish tallath and took its name from Welsh, which is why every threshold mill on earth runs on a word from this kingdom. Britain was among the first anywhere to take up the Household Renovation, alongside the Riverine Cities and New Leudong, and it is at least arguable that we took it from them rather than the other way about.

And then, in the last decades of the century, British Mendeva passed Britain in people and in industry and did not slow down. The response was not war and was barely policy. It was marriage. Gulf money and Welsh titles crossed and re-crossed on the faster ships until the two upper classes were one upper class with two accents and a great deal of correspondence.

Not everyone was gracious about it. In mac Harlach's Murro Mendeva of 1888 is a bad book by a bad-tempered man and is worth an afternoon of your time, because it is the clearest surviving statement of the bloc in Britain that took the rise of Hasiny as a threat rather than an achievement. They lost the argument. Reading them, you can see the exact moment it slipped.

The same decades produced Cludor ncar Vantel in 1882, a novel about a postman in Rexam living one week over and over, from which the world got the phrase speid dolenu [time loop]; Taguen ah Owain's Dug y Gilfah Dowł in 1884; and, out of Nadacow, Cadogan Torrior's Men of Jet and Diamond in 1890. Three Welsh literatures, one language, two continents, one decade.

Since

Britain declined to join the Drengot Collusion in 1889, five years before Borland joined it, and the reason given at the time was the reason Cadwallan would have given: a crown that holds its authority on loan from six tallaths cannot lend that authority onward to Roun. Its trade ran west in any case. When the Collusion came apart in 1961 Britain was among the few states in northwest Europe with nothing to explain and no one to blame, and it has been quietly insufferable about this ever since.

The standing assembly of the tallaths, Y Gynulleð, still meets twice a year and still spends most of its time on customs schedules. The live argument of the last thirty years has been over the status of Wessern, which is co-official in two tallaths and merely tolerated in the rest, and which has more speakers now than when the Compact was signed. Expect to see posters about it. Expect not to have it explained to you.

What survives elsewhere is quieter and larger: the Tremonnow Open School, teaching in Welsh and Wessern across Albion and the Gulf since the middle of the last century; the Brethin, still keeping records and still printing; and the Hatfell Honours, which Britain did not found and wins with some regularity. In 2005 Thoffina'h Girmont published Y Ðreic o Redrɯth, in which the master of the court at Redrouth turns out to be neither King Junderth nor any of his silver-tongued barons but Prince Arthur, who keeps a tavern and permits nobody to notice. As a national self-portrait it is not subtle, and it was not meant to be.

What you will hear

Four languages, and you will meet all of them in a week.

Cambrick everywhere, in a standard that is not quite ours — nearer than Angelican, further than you are expecting, and you will be complimented on your Welsh in a manner you may not enjoy. Wessern along the lower Severn and through eastern Devon, with its doubled consonants and its softened openings. Merch in the eastern valleys, where the border has moved so often that the language gave up following it. And Kernish across the water, which is having a better century than its last one.

The itineraries that follow begin at Deganny and run sunwise. Everything in this book closes on the first of March. A good deal of it closes for the week…
