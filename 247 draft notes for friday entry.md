247.5: The Four and the Shore

collected notes on Ireland and its polities.

Ireland (Borlish Irðon, Welsh Iwerðon) is the westernmost of the large Northwestern Isles, lying west of Albion [Great Britain] across the Irish Sea. It is divided between two polities. The Four Provinces, a federation of the old kingdoms of Tyrconnal, Connaught, Munster and Lenster, hold the west, the south and the north-west of the island. The east coast from the Wikinlow hills [Wicklow Mountains] north to Strangford Lough, together with the plains inland as far as Killdara [Kildare], forms the Shore, the Irish tallath [province] of the Kingdom of Britain and the most populous of Britain's outlying tallaths.

Kelts and Romans

Ireland's Iron Age began, as on the other Northwestern Isles, around the ninth century aN. Its people were Insular Kelts, whom Ptolemy appears to have described as a single group together with the peoples of Albion and Borland. That grouping was later divided, first by the English Strait at some point before Caesar's first invasion of Albion, and then by the Irish Sea.

The Roman Empire conquered the whole of Borland and part of Albion, but it never made the attempt on Ireland. Roman coin and glass nonetheless turn up in Lenster graves of the second and third centuries, having come across from the Roman shore of Albion in trade.

Saints and annals

The conversion of Ireland is credited to Patrick, a Briton carried to Ireland as a slave in his youth, who returned to it as a bishop in the fifth century. His feast on 17 March is the national day of the Four Provinces. The abbess Brigid founded her house at Killdara, whose name means "church of the oak" in Irish. Her cult crossed the Irish Sea even where Irish monks did not: the abbey at Rexam [Wrexham] in Wales whose original building burned in 1301, when Andrew II of Markland reignited the Mallor Wars, was dedicated to her.

Ireland's monasteries were comparatively insular. Rome under Pope Mercury II treated Borland as an outpost from which missionaries could be sent east and west, and Borland's houses looked outward accordingly. Ireland's looked inward. They sent out few missionaries, lent out few books, and kept annals in a tradition of their own. When the Brethin were founded at Tremonnow [Monmouth] in the eighth century under the patronage of Iseld, daughter of Vigo the Magnificent, their first charge was the history of the Christianisation of Albion and Ireland. For the Irish half they depended on what the Irish houses would let them copy, which for three centuries was very little.

The most important of the Irish annals is the Ultaugh Chronicle, kept at the monastery of Banchor [Bangor, Co. Down] in the Ultaugh, the old kingdom of the north-east. With the Chronicle of Carlile it is one of the two principal sources for the Dane centuries around the Irish Sea; both, for example, record the partition of Mondwell [Anglesey] agreed between Wynneth [Gwynedd] and Sodrick [Man and the Hebrides]. The two survive together, and only in copies made by the Brethin at Tremonnow. The Carlile chronicle was presumably ferried across the Irish Sea before the monastery at Carlile burned down around 1013, and it is generally supposed that it went to Banchor. When the Ultaugh broke apart a few years later, the Banchor community sent both books on to Tremonnow for safekeeping. It is the only occasion on which an Irish house is known to have let its annals leave the island.

The Danes

Viking raids on the shores of Albion and Ireland followed those on Borland, which had begun in the late eighth century. The first recorded raid on Albion was the sack of Halgrive in the summer of 824, and the Ultaugh Chronicle notes raids on the coast of the Ultaugh in the same decade.

Danes settled in Ireland during the Norrish ascendancy of the eighth and ninth centuries. Dublin became a Dane kingdom, and lesser Dane ports grew up at Wikinlow, Carlingford and Strangford. The wayports and settlements between Dublin and Yorwick [York] carried enough traffic that historians have credited it with giving the principality of Wynneth the resources to consolidate primacy over Powes [Powys], and later to become the core of a united Wales.

In the tenth century the Danes of Sodrick, weakened by famine at home and overextended in the east, lost much of their territory to Dunclothe. Under King Roderick, Dunclothe took Arran in 934 and Mann in 942. The expansion caused lasting acrimony with the Irish kingdoms as well as with Denmark, for the kings of the Ultaugh had taken tribute from Mann for generations and lost it with the island. Many of Sodrick's people fled or were expelled to Dane-ruled Yorwick, to Normandy and to Ireland, and Dublin grew sharply in these decades.

The breaking of the Ultaugh

The Ultaugh outlived the loss of Mann by eighty years. Squeezed between Tyrconnal to the west and the Dane ports on its own coast, it was broken apart in the years around 1020. Its last king, Oghy the Lame, was killed in 1021. Tyrconnal took the inland country as far as Lough Nay [Lough Neagh], Lenster took the southern marches, and the coast passed to the Danes of Strangford and Carlingford.

A mapbook of Albion and Ireland in 1028 accordingly shows "the ever-fractious kingdoms of Ireland" lately reduced to four: Tyrconnal, Connaught, Lenster and Munster. Tyrconnal and Lenster were fighting wars at once with the Norrish to the east and the other Irish to the west, while the houses of Connaught and Munster vied for the position of High King of Ireland. Dublin, meanwhile, belonged with Dunclothe and Sodrick to the confederation of Dane kingdoms in which Northumber was first among equals, all of them newly untethered from overseas rule.

Next to the Drengot Empire

The Kernovan Conjuring of 1029 put Roger Drengot of Normandy on the throne of Kent, and within ten years most of the land from Edinbrough to the Pyrenees was his. Northumber became the empire's northernmost vassal, and the confederation of the Danes came apart. Dublin, left without its partner across Albion, turned instead to the newly united Wales, as Dunclothe did. For the next century and a half the Dane ports of the Irish coast and the Welsh kingdom were allies of convenience against Drengot power, and the old road between Dublin and Yorwick gave way to the crossings between Dublin and Wales. The Drengot emperors never crossed the Irish Sea.

In the west the contest between Connaught and Munster for the High Kingship ran on through the eleventh century. In 1072 the Munster king Cormack mac Teague refused Connaught the customary exchange of hostages, and by the beginning of May Connaught was ready to declare war. The war was fought mostly along the Shannon and ended in 1074 at the ford of Ahlone [Athlone], where King Flann of Connaught was killed. Cormack (king of Munster 1068–1110) was the first High King in three generations whom all four kingdoms acknowledged. His house held the title, with interruptions, for most of the next two centuries from its seat at Cashel.

Under Cormack's successors the Brethin at last gained a foothold in Ireland. The Brethin of Kernow [Brittany] were at the height of their influence in the twelfth century, when many courts of Europe kept their monks as advisors, and in 1143 the High King's court at Cashel took in Brother Riwallon of Landvennick. The house he founded at Cashel was the first Brethin house in Ireland, and its copying-room gave the order regular access to the Irish annals for the first time.

The annals also show how slowly the island took to Pope Nuntius's reform of the calendar, which early in the twelfth century had moved the New Year from the Annunciation on 25 March to David's Day on 1 March. The Irish houses went on beginning the year at the Annunciation until the 1190s. For events between the two dates, therefore, the Irish reckoning runs a year behind that of Welsh and Borlish chroniclers, and reconciling the two is a standing exercise for students of the period.

The long thirteenth century

The Drengot Empire was dissolved in 1191. Wales spent the following century collecting counties and took Devon in 1280. In Ireland the period saw the Dane ports become Irish in speech. The Norrish of Dublin is last found in writing around 1240, but the lords of the coast went on giving their sons Norrish names for two centuries more, a pattern familiar from the Danelands of Albion as "father's name, mother's tongue".

Over the same century Tyrconnal absorbed the last independent lordships of the inland Ultaugh, and the Munster High Kings lost their hold over Lenster, which was ruled for most of the period by its own house from Kilkenny. The Connaught house took the High Kingship back in 1287 and held it, against Munster's repeated challenges, until the 1340s.

Salt and sail

In the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries the cod fisheries of Dunclothe sold their catch as far as the eastern Middlesea [Mediterranean], and the lords of the Irish Sea grew wealthy on the flow of salt north and fish south. Dublin, with the best harbour on the Irish side, became the sea's salt-market: salt from the pans of Kernow was landed, weighed and taxed there before going north to the curing-yards. By the early fifteenth century the lords of the coast, known by then simply as the Shore, had acquired the habit of thinking of themselves as having common political interests with the nobility of Wales, Dunclothe, Sodrick, Kernow and Devon.

The Compact and the League

When the oaths of the Tythew Compact were sworn at Tythew [St Davids] on David's Day 1424, the Shore was among the parties, its lords represented by Ottar mac Donnell of Dublin. Like the other tallaths it gave the new crown its navy, its coinage and its ambassadors, and kept its own courts, levies, taxes and languages. The Shore's courts accordingly still sit under the Dublin town law, a compound of Irish law and Dane custom, and the tallath's assembly still meets at the Thingmote, the mound outside Dublin where the Dane kings once held their assemblies.

The four Irish kings watched the Compact with alarm. In 1431 they met at Tarah [Tara], the ancient seat of the High Kings, and bound themselves into the League of Tarah. Its terms were a common peace among the four kingdoms, a common answer to any outside war, and a High King elected for life by the four kings from among their own houses, in place of the old contest of arms. The first king so elected was Fiach mac Nial of Tyrconnal (r. 1431–58). The League is the origin of the modern Four Provinces, which count their union from 1431.

The League kept out of the Wars of Fealty (1467–1513), as Borland did, and sold horses and salted beef to whoever would buy them. The Shore, as part of Britain, shared in the British gains of the war's early years and in Britain's early exit after the Battle of Rexam in 1483. King Howel spent the peace that followed on harbours, and Dublin's shipwrights had a large share of the work. The Phœbos, which reached Borune [Borneo] in 1516 and met the Morrack [Moroccan] ship Cynthia at Sinquan the following year, was commanded by Martan mac Kellot, and the Shore has long claimed him: Kellot is taken to be the Dublin form of the Norrish name Ketill. Nothing in the surviving records says where he was born.

The Second Great Dying reached Dublin in the spring of 1519 on the salt-ships. As elsewhere, coasts and ports were hit first and hardest, and the Shore lost perhaps a quarter of its people within five years. The League's records claim far lighter losses in the west, though they cannot be entirely trusted. To refill the coast, landlords of the Shore brought tenants and craftsmen across from Wales, and it is from these decades that Cambrick [Welsh], rather than Irish, became the common speech of Dublin.

The Killdara War

The Romantic Wars of the mid-seventeenth century are sometimes considered the last truly localised conflicts in Europe, and they had an Irish chapter in the Killdara War (1651–56). It began over the lordship of Killdara, which belonged to Lenster but had been mortgaged for a generation to Dublin salt-merchants. Munster came in on Lenster's side and the British navy on the Shore's. By the Treaty of Kilkenny (1656) Lenster ceded Killdara and the Mide plain [Meath] to the Shore, which roughly doubled in area. Welsh companies opened the lead and silver mines of the Killdara hills soon afterwards, and Welsh miners followed them.

In the same century British ships founded staddomains along the Arcabil Gulf [Gulf of Mexico], and Shore families were among their first settlers. The phrase "Irish Welsh", for the Cambrick-speaking families of the Shore, is first found in Gulf records of the 1680s.

The Charter of Tarah

The Democratic Wars of the mid-eighteenth century reached Ireland as they reached everywhere. In the League, a rising in 1758 against the High King, Donal of Munster, ended with the Charter of Tarah (1761), drafted largely by the Connaught lawyer Ferdia Hanlon. The Charter made the four kingdoms into provinces, each with an assembly of its own, under a common Assembly at Tarah. The High Kingship survived, but like the thrones of Madray and Nackon Day it came out of the wars with its powers sharply trammelled. The High King is still elected for life, now by the Assembly, and still from among the four royal houses.

Britain came out of the same wars a monarchy, its tallaths having sworn the Compact afresh, and the Shore's place in it was not seriously questioned.

Killdark

In January 1824 the assayer and alchemist Yorreth ap Trahaen isolated an unknown mineral, mẃn kildarec "killdark ore", from the mines at Killdara. A Dublin pottery maker, Una Kilbride, at first monopolised it for yellow-green glazes, and "Killdara yellow" ware was fashionable around the Irish Sea for a generation. The substance, killdark [uranium], is now the corporal [element] used in the vast majority of threshold mills [nuclear power plants]. Its name comes from Welsh kildárc "from Killdara", so that by way of Brigid's oak church every threshold mill on earth runs on a word from the Shore.

Britain led the Global Workshop period of the mid-nineteenth century. The Shore's share of it was shipbuilding at Dublin and Strangford, the potteries, and the Killdara mines. The Four Provinces, with little coal and fewer ports, sent people instead: emigration from the west went mostly to the Arcabil Gulf and to the towns of north-western Markland, among them Utter Safford [Greater Salford] and Runkhorn [Runcorn]. In 1888 the Irish Welshman In mac Harlach published The Making of Mendeva, the clearest surviving statement of the bloc in Britain that saw the rise of Hasiny as a threat.

The twentieth century

Britain declined to join the Drengot Collusion in 1889, and the Shore with it; the Four Provinces were never members either. After the Collusion collapsed in 1961, the Hatfell Honours were opened to artists across all of Gaul and the Northwestern Isles. In 1964 the poet Sorcha Neylan of Gallive [Galway] became the first laureate from the Four Provinces, for The Ultaugh Leaves, a verse cycle on the annalists of Banchor.

Ireland today

The Four Provinces are a federation of Tyrconnal, Connaught, Munster and Lenster, governed by the Assembly at Tarah under an elected High King. Irish is their official language. The Shore is the Irish tallath of Britain, with its own courts and its assembly at the Thingmote; Dublin, its capital, is the largest city on the island. Cambrick is the majority language of the Shore and Irish is co-official, spoken mostly in the inland districts ceded by Lenster in 1656.

Language

Irish is a Kelt language, related to Welsh and to the old Kelt language of Borland. Etymologists compare Irish mac "son" with Welsh magwr "cowherd" (formerly "breeder of animals"), frágh "heather" with Borland Kelt vrūg, and márah "tomorrow" with Welsh bore "morning" and Borland Kelt bora(g) "east, sunrise". Old Irish dún "fort" may lie behind the Dun- of Dunclothe.

Irish words have mostly reached Borlish at second hand, through Welsh. The best known is kildarc "killdark", from Welsh kildárc. The Borlish name of the island, Irðon, likewise comes through Welsh Iwerðon. The Norrish of the Dane ports survives in the place-names of the Shore (Wikinlow, Carlingford, Strangford) and in personal names such as Ottar and Kellot.

Customs

- Patrick's Day (17 March) is kept wherever the Irish settled. In Utter Safford and the other towns of north-western Markland it is the occasion of rival light shows, and the oldest of the crews still goes by the name of the Dubliners.
- Sawin, the Irish feast at the start of November, is kept in Markland under its Irish name.
- The Maritime card deck is common across Ireland and the rest of the Northwestern Isles, as in Denmark and Willemy, though it originates in fifteenth-century Poland.
- The Irish myth of Oil Breasail tells of a land west of Ireland, shrouded in mist except for one day every seven years. Islands labelled Brasil appear on late-medieval maps of the Atlantic, and the name almost certainly inspired that of Brasil in the Novomund.
- Lord Carrow's eighteenth-century mealbook A Complete Lithing of the Towns and Cities of Markland, for the Edification of the Good Food Amateur was popular across the Northwestern Isles, Ireland included, but failed to sell further afield.
