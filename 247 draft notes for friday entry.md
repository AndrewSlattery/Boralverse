Next to the Drengot Empire

The Kernovan Conjuring of 1029 put Roger Drengot of Normandy on the throne of Kent, and within ten years most of the land from Edinbrough to the Pyrenees was his. Northumber became the empire's northernmost vassal, and the confederation of the Danes came apart.

Dublin, left without its partner across Albion, turned instead to the newly united Wales, as Dunclothe did. For the next century and a half the Dane ports of the Irish coast and the Welsh kingdom were allies of convenience against Drengot power, and the old road between Dublin and Yorwick gave way to the crossings between Dublin and Wales. The Drengot emperors never crossed the Irish Sea.

In the west the contest between Connaught and Munster for the High Kingship ran on through the eleventh century. In 1072 the Munster king Cormack mac Teague refused Connaught the customary exchange of hostages, and by the beginning of May Connaught was ready to declare war. The war was fought mostly along the Shannon and ended in 1074 at the ford of Ahlone [Athlone], where King Flann of Connaught was killed. Cormack (king of Munster 1068–1110) was the first High King in three generations whom all four kingdoms acknowledged. His house held the title, with interruptions, for most of the next two centuries from its seat at Cashel.

Under Cormack's successors the Brethin at last gained a foothold in Ireland. The Brethin of Kernow [Brittany] were at the height of their influence in the twelfth century, when many courts of Europe kept their monks as advisors. In 1143 the High King's court at Cashel took in Brother Riwallon of Landvennick. The house he founded at Cashel was the first Brethin house in Ireland, and its copying-room gave the order regular access to the Irish annals for the first time.

The annals also show how slowly the island took to Pope Nuntius's reform of the calendar, which early in the twelfth century had moved the New Year from the Annunciation on 25 March to David's Day on 1 March. The Irish houses went on beginning the year at the Annunciation until the 1190s. For events between the two dates, therefore, the Irish reckoning runs a year behind that of Welsh and Borlish chroniclers. Reconciling the two is a standing exercise for students of the period.

The making of the Arvor

The Drengot Empire was dissolved in 1191, and Wales spent the following century collecting counties. The Dane ports of the Irish coast were among them. Their lords had looked to Wales for a century and a half, and in the 1270s, pressed from inland by Lenster, the lords of Dublin, Wikinlow, Carlingford and Strangford did homage one after another to King David IV. The Welsh chancery entered them on its rolls as yr Arvor, "the seaboard". Devon, which David declared a tallath in 1280 and granted whole to a single lord, came in by conquest. The Arvor came in county by county, and so became part of Wales itself rather than a tallath beside it.

In the same period the Dane ports became Irish in speech. The Norrish of Dublin is last found in writing around 1240, but the lords of the Arvor went on giving their sons Norrish names for two centuries more. The pattern is familiar from the Danelands of Albion as "father's name, mother's tongue".

Four kingdoms into two

Inland, the four kingdoms became two:
- Tyrconnal absorbed the last independent lordships of the inland Ultaugh over the thirteenth century.
- Lenster, cut off from the sea once the Arvor did homage to Wales, could not hold out against Munster. Its last king died without an heir in 1294, and the king of Munster took its crown.
- The Connaught house took the High Kingship back from Munster in 1287 and held it against Munster's repeated challenges until the 1340s.
- In 1448 Fiach mac Nial, king of Connaught, inherited Tyrconnal through his wife, and the two crowns were one by the end of the century.

From then on there were two Irish kingdoms. Munster held the south and the midlands, from Corcagh [Cork] to Killdara. Connaught held the west and the north, from Gallive [Galway] to Lough Nay.

Salt and sail

In the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries the cod fisheries of Dunclothe sold their catch as far as the eastern Middlesea [Mediterranean], and the lords of the Irish Sea grew wealthy on the flow of salt north and fish south. Dublin, with the best harbour on the Irish side, became the sea's salt-market: salt from the pans of Kernow was landed, weighed and taxed there before going north to the curing-yards. The nobility of the Arvor were by then thinking of themselves as having common political interests with those of the rest of Wales and of Dunclothe, Sodrick, Kernow and Devon.

When the oaths of the Tythew Compact were sworn at Tythew [St Davids] on David's Day 1424, the Arvor swore as part of Wales, and Ottar mac Donnell of Dublin was among the Welsh lords. The tallaths gave the new crown their navies, their coinage and their ambassadors, and kept their own courts, levies, taxes and languages. The Arvor, being Welsh counties, swore as Wales and not as a tallath. Dublin's city assembly nonetheless still meets at the Thingmote, the mound where the Dane kings once held theirs.

The two Irish kingdoms were not party to the Compact. They kept out of the Wars of Fealty (1467–1513), as Borland did, and sold horses and salted beef to whoever would buy them.

King Howel spent the peace after 1483 on harbours, and Dublin's shipwrights had a large share of the work. The Phœbos, which reached Borune [Borneo] in 1516 and met the Morrack [Moroccan] ship Cynthia at Sinquan the following year, was commanded by Martan mac Kellot. The Arvor has long claimed him, taking Kellot to be the Dublin form of the Norrish name Ketill, but nothing in the surviving records says where he was born.

The Second Great Dying reached Dublin in the spring of 1519 on the salt-ships. As elsewhere, coasts and ports were hit first and hardest. The Arvor lost perhaps a quarter of its people within five years, and Munster's southern ports at Corcagh and Wadderford [Waterford] suffered nearly as badly. Connaught's records claim far lighter losses, though they cannot be entirely trusted. To refill the coast, landlords of the Arvor brought tenants and craftsmen across from the rest of Wales, and it is from these decades that Cambrick [Welsh], rather than Irish, became the common speech of Dublin.

Munster joins Britain

Munster came out of the plague years depleted and facing a Connaught that had suffered far less. On David's Day 1533 its king, Mortagh of Cashel, swore the Compact at Tythew, and Munster became the first Irish tallath. It took the same terms as the other tallaths, with one addition the older ones had not needed: Munster kept its king, who reigns over the tallath under the British crown, where the other tallaths have lords.

The War of the Shannon

The Romantic Wars of the mid-seventeenth century are sometimes considered the last truly localised conflicts in Europe. Their Irish chapter was the War of the Shannon (1649–55) between Connaught and Munster, fought over the midland lordships between the Shannon and the Killdara hills, which both kingdoms claimed. The British navy held the Shannon estuary for Munster. In 1655 Connaught, unable to win and unwilling to be conquered, came to terms under its king, Cathal of Crogan.

By the Treaty of Ahlone, Connaught:
- swore the Compact as the second Irish tallath;
- kept its king, as Munster had;
- gave up the title of High King of Ireland, which its kings had claimed alone since Munster's oath;
- won a term no other tallath had, that its levies could not be sent out of Ireland.

After the war, Welsh companies opened the lead and silver mines of the Killdara hills under licence from Munster, and Welsh miners followed them.

In the same century British ships founded staddomains along the Arcabil Gulf [Gulf of Mexico], and families from the Arvor were among their first settlers. The phrase "Irish Welsh", for the Cambrick-speaking families of the Arvor, is first found in Gulf records of the 1680s.

The Irish Articles

The Democratic Wars of the mid-eighteenth century reached Ireland as they reached everywhere. Britain came out of them a monarchy, its tallaths having sworn the Compact afresh, and in that bargaining the two Irish tallaths drove the hardest terms. The Irish Articles (1761), drafted largely by the Connaught lawyer Ferdia Hanlon, gave Munster and Connaught elected assemblies, at Cashel and at Gallive, which choose their own ministers. Like the kings of Madray and Nackon Day, the kings of Munster and Connaught kept their thrones but saw their powers sharply trammelled. The Articles are the main reason the Irish tallaths are counted the most loosely held in Britain.

Killdark

In January 1824 the assayer and alchemist Yorreth ap Trahaen isolated an unknown mineral, mẃn kildarec "killdark ore", from the mines at Killdara in Munster. A Dublin pottery maker, Una Kilbride, at first monopolised it for yellow-green glazes, and "Killdara yellow" ware was fashionable around the Irish Sea for a generation. The substance, killdark [uranium], is now the corporal [element] used in the vast majority of threshold mills [nuclear power plants]. Its name comes from Welsh kildárc "from Killdara", and so ultimately from Brigid's church of the oak.

Britain led the Global Workshop period of the mid-nineteenth century. The Arvor's share of it was shipbuilding at Dublin and Strangford and the potteries, and Munster's was the Killdara mines. Connaught, with little coal, sent people instead. Emigration from both Irish tallaths went mostly to the Arcabil Gulf and to the towns of north-western Markland, among them Utter Safford [Greater Salford] and Runkhorn [Runcorn]. In 1888 the Irish Welshman In mac Harlach published The Making of Mendeva, the clearest surviving statement of the bloc in Britain that saw the rise of Hasiny as a threat.

The twentieth century

Britain declined to join the Drengot Collusion in 1889, so no part of Ireland was ever a member. After the Collusion collapsed in 1961, the Hatfell Honours were opened to artists across all of Gaul and the Northwestern Isles. In 1964 the poet Sorcha Neylan of Gallive became the first laureate writing in Irish, for The Ultaugh Leaves, a verse cycle on the annalists of Banchor.

Ireland today

The Arvor is part of Wales, in the four counties of Dublin, Wikinlow, Carlingford and Strangford. Cambrick is its majority language and Irish a minority one.

Munster and Connaught are outlying tallaths of Britain. Each has its own king, its own assembly (at Cashel and at Gallive respectively) and its own courts. Irish is the official language of both. Between them they are Britain's most populous outlying tallaths.

Language

Irish, also called Guile (the name it shares with the Gaelic of Scotland), is a Kelt language, related to Welsh and to the old Kelt language of Borland. Its speakers call the island Éire. Etymologists compare:
- Irish mac "son" with Welsh magwr "cowherd" (formerly "breeder of animals");
- frágh "heather" with Borland Kelt vrūg;
- márah "tomorrow" with Welsh bore "morning" and Borland Kelt bora(g) "east, sunrise".

Old Irish dún "fort" may lie behind the Dun- of Dunclothe.

Irish words have mostly reached Borlish at second hand, through Welsh. The best known is kildarc "killdark", from Welsh kildárc. The Borlish name of the island, Irðon, likewise comes through Welsh Iwerðon, and Borlish writes Connaught as Connagt. The Norrish of the Dane ports survives in the place-names of the Arvor (Wikinlow, Carlingford, Strangford) and in personal names such as Ottar and Kellot.

Customs

- Patrick's Day (17 March) is kept wherever the Irish settled. In Utter Safford and the other towns of north-western Markland it is the occasion of rival light shows, and the oldest of the crews still goes by the name of the Dubliners.
- Sawin, the Irish feast at the start of November, is kept in Markland under its Irish name.
- The Maritime card deck originates in fifteenth-century Poland, but it is common across Ireland and the rest of the Northwestern Isles, as it is in Denmark and Willemy.
- The Irish myth of Oil Breasail tells of a land west of Ireland, shrouded in mist except for one day every seven years. Islands labelled Brasil appear on late-medieval maps of the Atlantic, and the name almost certainly inspired that of Brasil in the Novomund.
- Lord Carrow's eighteenth-century mealbook A Complete Lithing of the Towns and Cities of Markland, for the Edification of the Good Food Amateur was popular across the Northwestern Isles, Ireland included, but failed to sell further afield.
