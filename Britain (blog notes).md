Working notes toward 237.5. Compiled from the Obsidian vault, `Data/output_directory/`,
`Data/pre-2022 notes.txt`, `borlish sentences with translations.txt`, `all_claims_final.yaml`
and `World 2026 - Canon Survey.md`.

## 0. The headline

**Britain is the largest polity in the setting that has never had a `.5` entry.**

- It is named in **49 of the 234 notes files**, **12 excerpts** and **3 dictionary entries** —
  more than Denmark, Provence, Willemy, Morrack or Tavancy, all of which have entries.
- `Mapbook/Britain.md` exists and is substantial.
- The vault page's full text was **appended verbatim to `notes/97.5_entrcollocq_interview.md`**
  as raw material and then never written up. It has been sitting in the corpus, unused, since
  post 97.

**And it fits the brief exactly.** Of the 33 notes entries that mention Britain by name,
**25 are pre-200**. The braid is already there; nothing needs inventing to connect it.

One further point in its favour: **every statement canon makes about Britain stops in the
nineteenth century.** The Canon Survey marks it ● (attested modern), but the only modern facts
are linguistic and cultural. Its twentieth century is empty. See §5.

## 1. What Britain is

> "Britain (Cambrick **Prydein**) is a polity of [[Albion]] centred on [[Wales]] but extending
> through [[Devon]] and [[Dunclothe]] and out to [[Kernow]] and parts of [[Ireland]]."
> — `Mapbook/Britain.md:1` (= the block appended to `notes/97.5`)

**Constituent parts.** Wales is the heartland; the outlying provinces are **tallaths**
(`taleð` in Borlish — see the sentence corpus at `borlish sentences with translations.txt:2451`,
where the Borlish renders "Wales and its outlying tallaths" as *Cambry e sy taleð fragossau*).

| Tallath | Entered | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Devon** | C13 by conquest; tallath declared by David IV | `notes/63.5` |
| **Dunclothe** | C15, at the Kingdom's founding | `notes/161.5:19` |
| **Sodrick** [Man and the Hebrides] | C15, "as an outlying tallath… when it was formed" | `Mapbook/Sodrick.md:13` |
| **Kernow** [Brittany] | C15, "as it incorporated territories further afield" | `notes/135.5:15` |
| **parts of Ireland** | unstated | `Mapbook/Britain.md:1` |

**Languages.** Cambrick (Welsh) is the state language. **Wessern** is a recognised minority
English variety:

> "the English language variety spoken as a recognised minority language in Britain [Wales,
> Devon and Cornwall, Brittany, Cumbria, Strathclyde, Man and the Isles, parts of Ireland],
> mostly in its eastern regions bordering Markland and Kent."
> — `notes/142.5`, footnote 2

That footnote is the single most useful line in the corpus for this entry: **it defines
Britain's full extent** in real-world terms, and it establishes that the Kingdom is
constitutionally bilingual along its eastern edge.

**Three Welsh standards survive** — British, Angelican and Hasinick — which the Canon Survey
correctly reads as proof that all three polities persist to 2026 (`World 2026 - Canon Survey.md:105`;
`notes/150.5`, `notes/118.5`).

## 2. The chronological spine

Every line below is attested. Dates in **N** are Nuntial.

### Before the Kingdom — Greater Devon and Wales

- **656** — Battle of Gaulford; the whole Devon peninsula falls under sole Wessex authority,
  "although the westernmost region is thought never to have fully submitted" (`notes/63.5`, `notes/40.5`).
- **720** — Queen Maud of Angeus dies; her son **Vigo** succeeds, shortly after the
  **Catumagin Accord** fixes the border with the Franks (`notes/135.5`).
- **c. 725** — Vigo marries **Winmar** (vault: *Guinnumar*), daughter of **David I of Wynneth**;
  "this marriage is likely what allowed him to secure Welsh assistance" (`notes/18.5`).
- **C8** — **Vigo the Magnificent** reconquers Devon; two decades of intermittent war end at
  **Petherton**; he is crowned first king of Greater Devon (*rex Magnæ Dumnoniæ*), uniting Devon
  with Kernow (`notes/63.5`).
- **before 800 N** — the **Brethin** are founded at **Tremonnow** under the patronage of Vigo's
  daughter **Iseld**. Their archives become the **Tremonnow Gravatory**, which holds "invaluable
  sources on early medieval Albion (including a copy of the **Chronicle of Carlile**)" (`notes/142.5`).
- **824** — Viking raids begin in Albion with the destruction of **Halgrive** (`notes/40.5`).
- **888 N** — the monastery at **Landvennick** in Kernow burned to the ground (`notes/135.5`).
- **928** — Dunclothe loses **Eidin** [Edinburgh] to Northumber (`notes/161.5`).
- **934, 942** — Dunclothe under **Roderick** takes Arran and then Man from the Danes of Sodrick
  (`notes/161.5`, `Mapbook/Sodrick.md`).
- **c. 1000** — Wales is unified (`notes/40.5`).
- **1028 / 1029** — the **Kernovan Conjuring**: the House of Kernow backs Roger Drengot's claim
  to Kent in exchange for Greater Devon. Proximate cause of the First Drengot Empire
  (`notes/116.5`, `notes/63.5` — **see the date flag in §6**).
- **C11** — **Cadwallan**, erstwhile King of Wynneth, keeps newly-united Wales "mostly free of
  Drengot control… by steering a course between Greater Devon and the Drengot, neither of whom
  wanted the other to have dominion over Wales" (`notes/116.5`). **This is the founding
  strategic posture of the state**, three and a half centuries early.
- **1225** — the **Realignment**: Devon becomes de facto independent of Kernow under a cadet
  branch (`notes/63.5`).
- **c. 1280** — **Ivan of Cartaw** conquers Devon; **David IV of Wales** declares the Tallath of
  Devon and awards it to Ivan's son **Marduth** (`notes/63.5`, `notes/198.5`).
- **1301** — **Andrew II of Markland** sacks Rexam / Car Vantel, reigniting the **Mallor Wars**;
  the original St Brigid's abbey burns (`notes/111.5`; `borlish sentences:391`).
- **C14–C15** — **Dunclothe-led North Atlantic fisheries** export dried and cured cod via the
  Borlish and Vascon meshes as far as the eastern Middlesea: "a notable source of specie for the
  vicinity" (`notes/161.5`).

### The Kingdom

- **C15** — the **Kingdom of Britain** is founded, "formalising the suzerainty of Wales over the
  tallaths of Devon and Dunclothe" (`Mapbook/Britain.md`, `notes/161.5`).
- **late C15 – early C16** — the **Wars of Fealty**, forty-six years (`notes/40.5`) or "almost
  fifty" (`notes/33.5`), triggered by a dispute over the **Redstone Chair**. Britain "started
  from a position of strength, and managed to extract many concessions in the early years."
- **1483** — the **Battle of Rexam**. The last **Duke of Chestre** is killed; Britain takes
  lasting control of the Rexam area, though the follow-up attack on Chester is rebuffed.
  **Britain then recuses itself from the remaining thirty years of the war** (`notes/111.5`, `notes/33.5`).
- **early C16** — **King Howel's** resupply ports down the west African coast, built as
  infrastructure for a direct Europe–Asia sea route (`notes/33.5`; `pre-2022:4605`).
- **1516–17** — the **Phœbos**, captained by **Martan mac Kellot**, rounds Africa, calls at
  **Ungotia**, is directed onward by Omani and then Chinese traders via **Gurjat** and **Madray**,
  and reaches **Borune** for the **Great Borunesk Meeting** of 1517. It meets the Morrack
  **Cynthia** at Sinquan. "At least a dozen crewmembers of the Phœbos spoke some Arabic, as the
  British and Morrack trade meshes had overlapped for centuries." mac Kellot declines to attempt
  circumnavigation (`notes/100.5`, `notes/233.5`).
- **1518–25** — the **Second Great Dying** finds Albion exhausted. "Almost twice as many deaths
  per capita are recorded in Markland and English Kent compared with Norman Kent or Willemy"
  (`notes/33.5`). Britain's advantage compounds.
- **C17** — British staddomains along the **Mendevan Gulf**, which become **British Mendeva**
  (`notes/102.5`).
- **1621** — the **Becouin Treaty** governs Santrafew's relations with the Mendevan peoples;
  it is broken by the Provincial settlers after the **British Sack of Santrafew**, "in which the
  Mastoisèt in particular were alleged to have collaborated" (`notes/60.5`, `excerpt/111.1`).
- **after the Angelican Landfall** — emigration from Britain, alongside Cathayan state-building,
  helps form **New Leudong** (`Mapbook/Britain.md`).
- **mid-C19** — the **Glamour of Deganny** records the British underworld slang that gives Borlish
  **vlangr** "fence" — a chain running Suahile *mlanguri* "healer" → Ongutian argot "profiteer" →
  British underworld slang → Borlish (`dictionary/216.2`).
- **1870** — **A Cyclopædia of British Persons** (third edition), by **Wolfston Potter**,
  published by the **Yievle Brethin Primers** (`Mapbook/Britain.md:16`). The sole cited source
  on the vault page.
- **late C19** — **British Mendeva overtakes Britain in population and industry.** Britain keeps
  cultural cachet, sustained by "migration of many members of the 'new rich' across the Atlantic,
  and the resultant intertwining of the respective upper-class societies through marriage."
  Whether this accelerated the export of **Household Renovation** mores is explicitly "debated"
  (`Mapbook/Britain.md`, `notes/102.5`).
- **mid-C20** — the **Tremonnow Open School**, formerly the Brethin Mesh Institute, provides
  distance learning in Welsh and Wessern "across Albion and, latterly, also across British
  Mendeva" (`notes/142.5`).
- **1991–95** — a restored steamship of the **Culombálu** fleet is displayed at **Deganny**
  (`notes/146.5`, quiz question 10).

## 4. The one line of attitude in the corpus

From *Daughters of the Sea: The Northwestern Isles*, a Merch-language history — i.e. written
from Markland, Britain's thousand-year rival:

> "Our neighbours to the east have never matched the globe-spanning workshop and household
> vanguard of the British."
> — `notes/25.5`, `excerpt/44.1`

"Our neighbours to the east" is Borland. So a Markish author, writing for a Markish audience,
concedes that Britain leads the world in industry *and* in the Household Renovation. That is the
tone the setting takes toward Britain, and it is available to be quoted, echoed or contradicted.

## 5. The modern blank

**Britain has no twentieth century in the corpus.** Specifically:

- **It is never named as a member of the Drengot Collusion.** The Collusion's political centres
  are London, Paris and Roun; Borland acceded in 1894; Vascony and Provence stood outside to the
  south; Portingale sat in its orbit (`notes/34.5`). Britain — directly west of London, and the
  largest polity on Albion by area — is simply never mentioned in connection with it, in either
  direction.
- **No Millstone War role.** No Good Game alliance. No Workshop-period industry beyond the Merch
  aside above.
- **No LGT entry**, despite Hasiny hosting the fourth tourney in 1935 (`notes/118.5`, `notes/207.5`).
- **No modern institutions** beyond the Tremonnow Open School and the Yievle Primers.
- The Canon Survey's blank #10 covers the neighbours: "**Northumber, Scotland and Ireland in the
  modern day** — all three named as polities/regions but with no post-medieval political
  statement." Britain "holds parts of Ireland", so any modern statement about Britain touches
  this blank whether or not it means to.

> **Reading (not canon).** The medieval posture — Cadwallan steering between two larger powers so
> that neither could hold Wales — is the same posture as the 1483 recusal from the Wars of Fealty,
> and the same posture as absence from the Collusion. Britain's characteristic move in this
> setting is **to take the early win and then leave the table.** That reading costs nothing, it is
> consistent with every attested episode, and it explains the modern silence rather than
> contradicting it. It is also the natural spine for an entry.

## 7. Decisions the entry will have to make

1. **How far into the modern era to go.** The safest high-value choice is to *end* on the modern
   silence rather than fill it — but if the entry does fill it, the Collusion question is the one
   readers will notice.
2. **Whether Britain is a monarchy in 2026.** Borland's monarchy fell in 1894; London still has a
   queen. Britain's is unstated after Howel and David IV.
3. **Ireland.** "Parts of Ireland" is the vaguest phrase on the vault page and the entry will be
   read as adjudicating it. Either name the parts or visibly decline.
4. **Whether to touch the African resupply ports.** They belong to Britain (§2, early C16) and they
   are the founding layer of the Atlantic Africa dossier's argument. Using them here is fine and
   arguably good setup — but leave the staddomains and the Culombálu fleet for that entry.
5. **How much Devon.** `excerpt/233.1 Medieval Devon` is four posts old. Vigo and Petherton should
   be a clause, not a paragraph.

## 8. Format proposal — no apparatus

The last two entries (`235.5`, `236.5`) both hang on a `===` block with numbered footnotes.
Three candidates that carry none, in order of preference.

### Primary: a guidebook to Britain published in Hasiny

A travel companion for British Mendevan readers visiting the old country — late C20 or
contemporary, written in Hasinick Welsh and given in translation.

- **Footnote-free by genre.** Guidebooks carry their asides inline: a parenthesis, a practical
  note, a warning about opening hours, a sentence of history per stop.
- **The document's audience *is* the thesis.** Canon's central claim about Britain is that its
  Novomundane child outgrew it in population and industry while it kept the cultural cachet
  (`notes/102.5`). A Hasinick author explaining Britain to Hasinick readers *performs* that
  relationship instead of asserting it — including the faint condescension of the richer party
  and the pilgrimage instinct of the poorer one, at the same time.
- **It is a natural container for a polity's whole history.** One stop per period: Rexam for 1483,
  Tremonnow for the Brethin and the Gravatory, Cartaw for the C13, Mondwell, Deganny for the sea
  and the underworld, Landvennick for the Vikings, the Dunclothe coast for the cod. The braid in
  §3 falls out of the itinerary rather than being marched through.
- **A register the blog has never used**: second person, present tense, practical, opinionated.
- It supplies the modern layer (§5) obliquely — what a visitor can see, get to, and book — without
  committing to a constitutional statement.
- The three Welsh standards become a live problem for the traveller rather than a linguistic note.

> Watch: keep it discursive per stop rather than a dated calendar, or it drifts toward the
> register of `196.5 Alster Almanac`.

### Alternative A: preface to the fourth edition of *A Cyclopædia of British Persons*

A later editor explaining what has had to change since Wolfston Potter's third edition of 1870.

- Canon-named source, already cited as *the* reference for Britain (`Mapbook/Britain.md:16`).
- A preface is structurally footnote-free, and a history of a polity told through **what a
  reference work stopped being able to say** is a genuinely novel move for the blog.
- Handles the modern blank elegantly: the editor can be explicit about which entries are now
  contested, which were quietly national myth, and which the Mendevan reading public no longer
  needs explained.
- Risk: it is one abstraction away from the subject, and it shares DNA with the Concise Onomatory
  afterwords (`227.5`, `234.5`).

### Alternative B: the Chronicle of Carlile

Annals, year by year, from the copy canon places in the Tremonnow Gravatory (`notes/142.5`).

- Wholly without apparatus; a register the blog has never used; and it would put the Brethin's
  record-keeping vocation on the page for the first time since `23.5`.
- **Fails the brief on span**: annals can only reach the medieval material, so the Kingdom itself,
  the Phœbos, British Mendeva and the modern blank all fall outside. Best kept for a
  Brethin-focused entry, or used as the `.1` excerpt to a future post.
