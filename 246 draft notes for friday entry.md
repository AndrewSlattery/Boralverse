246.5: The Pocket Vetomund

entries from The Pocket Vetomund, a single-volume encyclopedia of European history and places. It was first published in 1934 by Winchester Scholastic Primers, the press behind The Alphabetical Albion, and the entries below are taken from its fourth edition (1996).

HULM, BATTLE OF (1821)
An offensive of the Second German War (1815–23), fought at Hulm [Ulm] and remembered chiefly for its mismanagement. On one side fought Bavarn and its allies, among them Borland, Crovatia and Furlain; Borland's part in the war, and especially its alliance with the Northern Concert, had already provoked mozard protests in cities across that country. Furlain's presence at Hulm is notable, since for most of the war its troops fought principally against Bavarn. The most quoted verdict on the battle comes from the Borlish mathematician Darren Brodus, who served there and wrote in a letter of 1824 that it was "an astonishingly-mismanaged offensive" which "in all honesty prolonged the war for months". Among his comrades was the Furlain landowner Jacomo Bruno, twin brother of the composer Chiarina Bruno, with whom Brodus kept up a lifelong correspondence. The city itself is better known for its university, which has held since 1770 the first true self-describing book, duplicated once every four years, and which in 1819 published Desselut's posthumous *The Tellure and the Tellard*. See also BRUNO, CHIARINA; SECOND GERMAN WAR.

LITHOW
(Borlish *Lithou*) A land on the eastern coast of the Graven Sea [Baltic]. It was the first target of the Baptism of the North in the thirteenth century, when Rome, fearing that the Augsburg See would reach the north first, sent forces from Crain and Borland, with Borlish missionaries and the Convoy Australier, which later founded a chapter there. The eighteenth-century Lithowan Marion Hartman is remembered for helping to end the Russian War for the Graven, and by botanists for diagrams that label each case's nucleus *punctulus* "little dot". In 1859 Tomas Vilmanas designed the Vilmanas Teleclavia in Lithow, the first tachygraph [typewriter] able to send messages on the steeplemesh; its first message, the opening line of Ovid's *Heroines*, went to Margreta Legaite in Tvaniste, who had to receive it by hand. Lithowan *sudrabs* "silver" is cited as evidence that the word was an ancient trade-word, and *stabá* "idol" as a cousin of the folkloric *stobor*. See also BAPTISM OF THE NORTH; NAZIUNAS, AGATA.

AUGSBURG SEE
The papal seat at Augsburg that broke from Rome in the German Secession. In 1225 Paul von Burchardt, a friend of the city's mayor Wilhelm Brüz, wrote the Pauline Denunciations and declared a *sedes sanctissima* in Augsburg, reigning as Paul I/XI; the double number reflects a thirteenth-century dispute over whether the Roman popes should be counted, settled in the negative by Damien I. The duchies of the First German Empire each declared for Rome or for Augsburg, and the resulting patchwork brought the empire down. One Denunciation attacked Pope Nuntius's move of the New Year to 1 March as "the reckless diminution of our ordained measure", and the Augsburg dominions kept the Julian calendar until the Rhine Compromises. The archbishop of Waduz [Vaduz], a minor city of Bavarn, is the only official entitled to vote in the conclaves of both popes; none has yet been called for both at once. See also GERMAN SECESSION; PAULINE DENUNCIATIONS.

RODULF OF KENT (d. c. 1029)
King of Kent for nearly eight decades, having come to the throne as a child. Under him Kent, ruled from Canterbury, subdued Wessex (apart from Anglia, already lost to Markland in the Treaty of Oxford) and held a recognised supremacy over the rump Wessex and over Essex, though Devon contested the former. His long deathbed in 1028 left the succession deeply uncertain, and his death triggered the Kernovan Conjuring of 1029, in which the Drengots of Normandy conquered Kent with the help of Greater Devon's House of Kernow: the first step towards the First Drengot Empire. See also KERNOVAN CONJURING.
