# Atlantic Africa — Research Dossier

Working notes toward a future .5 entry. Compiled from the Obsidian vault, `Scaunç.txt`,
`Data/output_directory/`, `Data/pre-2022 notes.txt`, `borlish sentences with translations.txt`,
`all_claims_final.yaml` and `World 2026 - Canon Survey.md`.

**Same discipline as the Canon Survey: this document invents nothing.** Everything in §1–§5 is
traceable to a cited line. Inference is confined to §6–§8 and is labelled as such.

Status marks: **●** attested in the modern era · **◐** attested only pre-modern · **○** name only.

## 0. The headline

Canon states **twice**, in two independent places, that there are African polities descended from
trading posts — and **never names one**.

> "Through this period, Borlish trade guilds were involved in the establishment of staddomains along
> Atlantic [[Cappatia]] and **[[Africa]]**. Initially little more than trading posts, these would grow into
> significant polities over the next centuries."
> — `Mapbook/Borland.md:96` (= note 26.5), in the section headed **"The 17th and 18th centuries"**

> "**staddenzan** — staddenzen, local inhabitant of one of the polities that grew from early trading
> posts along the coasts of Cappatia [South America] **and Africa**; a direct borrowing from Saxon
> *staddenzen*, respelt to align with existing Boral *denzan* 'local, denizen, citizen'."
> — `Data/pre-2022 notes.txt:7831`

Two further facts sharpen this into a usable frame:

- The `[[Africa]]` wiki-link in `Mapbook/Borland.md` **points at a file that does not exist.** There is no
  `Mapbook/Africa.md`. The continent is linked and never written.
- `Mapbook/Europe.md` lists Morrack and the Single Caliphate with an asterisk — "only partly in Europe" —
  which is the whole of Africa's representation in the Mapbook indices.

So the founding date is fixed (C17–C18), the founding agent is fixed (**Borlish trade guilds**), the
outcome is fixed ("significant polities"), and every name is free.

## 1. Named African entities — the complete inventory

That is the entire continent.

| Name | | Where | What canon says |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Morrack** | ● | NW Africa [Morocco] | The best-documented African power by far. See §2. |
| **Libya** | ● | Middlesea coast, Morrack→Egypt | Formerly **Ifricia**, "an important admiralty of the Single Caliphate, ruled from **Tunis**"; independent admiralty declared **1237**, at roughly the same time as the Morrove admiralty to the west (`excerpt/119.1_Libya.md`). Its C17 court under **Hisham Ar-Rashid** gave political theory the terms *copperplate* and *tinplate*, via David Bensaíd's *The Three Cities* (1751). **In the modern day a significant refiner and exporter of cuproses** [sulphates] (`Mapbook/Libya.md:10`). |
| **Egypt** | ◐ | — | **Cairo** was the seat of the Single Caliphate, "primarily after the centralisation reforms of the early eleventh century" (`pre-2022:3503`). **Iscandar** [Alexandria] anchored the Riyalid Sultanate in the C17. Modern status unstated. |
| **Bamackon** | ◐ | W Africa | "A major population centre in 14th-century western Africa, **suzerain in particular over parts of the coast to its south**" (`pre-2022:7094`). | **Sant Adrian** | ◐ | [Bioko] | **Vascon-controlled from the C15** — the southern limit of Vascony's African coastal exploration. Still Vascon-held in the C17, when it was the western terminus of the Gurjat trade mesh. **The one named Atlantic-African possession in the corpus.** |
| **Hadramot** | ◐ | "Yemen and Ethiopia" | "A loose collection of wealthy city states and admiraltys controlling trade through the Gulf of Aden." Took Moscat from the Ibadine Imamate in **1652**. |

**Everything else is blank**: the Sahara, the entire Guinea coast, the Congo, the Cape, Madagascar,
East Africa between Ungotia and Caddisow, and the Atlantic-African staddomains themselves.

## 2. The external powers, in order of arrival

This is the sequence the entry can lean on. Every step is canon.

**C11–C13 — the Single Caliphate.** Ruled from **Cairo**; stretched "from Spain to Persia"; one of the four
empires of the **Second Tetrarchy**. Its African admiralties fragment in **1237**: Libya (Ifricia) and the
**Morrove admiralty** [the Maghreb] declare independence in the same year, "as imperial hegemony in
Europe and northern Africa began to fall apart."

**C14 — Bamackon.** The one attested indigenous West African power, "suzerain over parts of the coast to
its south," exporting kola and bananas north through the Amagre Gap into the Middlesea trade.

**C15 — Vascony.** "Being a maritime power, Vascony had explored parts of the African coast (as far south
as **Sant Adrian** [Bioko]) during the fifteenth century" (`Mapbook/Vascony.md`). Then the brake:
Vascony "lost over a fifth of its population to the Second Great Dying" (1518–25) — for the same maritime
reason. **The Vascon push down the African coast stalls at Bioko and never resumes.**

**C15–C16 — Morrack.** Munir al-Hamdawi founds the **New World Company** (شركة العالم الجديد) in **1463**;
Morracan ships make first Vetomundane contact with the Arcabil, Mashick and Tavancy, and are **first to
circumnavigate the world**, reaching the Great Borunesk Meeting of 1517 *via Cappatia*. Ports: **Gadire**
[Agadir], **Tanger**, **Anfa** [Casablanca], **X̀r l Joas** [Ksar es-Seghir]. Note the direction of travel —
Morrack's imperial energy goes **west**, not south.

**C16 — Britain.** The decisive Atlantic-African line, and it is easy to miss:

> "a notable exception was the relatively-new Kingdom of Britain, where **Howel's ships had already set up
> resupply ports along the African coast**. Having profited from the ongoing Wars of Fealty… the king was
> convinced more opportunity lay in connecting the European market directly to [Asia]"
> — `pre-2022:4605`, on the run-up to the *Phœbos* voyage

So Britain — the Wales-centred kingdom — has a **chain of victualling stations down the Atlantic African
coast from the early C16**, established as infrastructure for the eastward Asia run, not as an end in
itself. The *Phœbos* then "rounds Africa" and makes port at Ungotia.

**C17–C18 — the Borlish trade guilds.** The staddomains (§0). This is a *later* wave than Britain's
resupply ports and a *commercial* rather than royal enterprise — Borland's characteristic form.

**C17 — Gurjat.** Its trade mesh spans "from African ports like Ungotia and even Vascon-controlled Sant
Adrian in the west to Cathay in the east" (`Mapbook/Gurjat.md`). African ports are inside an **Indian**
commercial system a century before they are inside a northern European one.

**mid-C19 — the "opening" of Caddisow.** The only named African event of the modern period, and it is
dated by its *effect on European genre fiction*, which is a very Boralverse way to date something.

> **Reading (not canon).** The shape here is unusually clean and worth preserving: Africa is entered by
> **four different powers for four different reasons** — Cairo administratively, Vascony exploratively
> (and abortively), Britain logistically, Borland commercially — with Morrack, the nearest great power,
> facing away the whole time. Nobody in canon goes to Atlantic Africa *for Atlantic Africa*. That is a
> ready-made explanation for why the staddomains were left alone long enough to become polities.

## 3. The Culombálu dossier

Two attestations. **Both are cut off exactly where the polity would be named.**

**(a)** `Scaunç.txt:7301` — a cutting from **Fiellas Dimenja**, 17 March 1996, the Marsella weekly:

> "…first steamship from the great **Culombálu** fleet proceed on schedule, and it will be open to public
> viewings from January this year."

**(b)** `notes/146.5:43` — *Excourse Puzzlers*, Royal Tommarth Castinghouse, 2021, Twentieth Century
History II, question 10:

> "The restored steamship displayed from 1991 to 1995 in **Deganny** [Deganwy, Wales] was originally part
> of what polity's '**Culombálu**' fleet?"

### What these two lines constrain

- **Culombálu is the name of a fleet, and the fleet belongs to a polity that is not named.** The quiz
  treats the answer as common knowledge; the scare quotes mark *Culombálu* as a word being quoted from
  another language.
- **The fleet is old enough and large enough to be "great" and to have a "first steamship"** — i.e. it
  predates steam and converted to it. A named fleet with a pre-steam history implies a chartered
  trading marine, not a navy.
- **A vessel from it spent 1991–95 on display in Wales**, then — on the 1996 reading — was restored and
  opened to public viewing elsewhere in January 1996. The Deganny display **ends** the year before the
  new viewings **begin**.
- **The orthography is not Borlish.** Borlish has ‹ç ð æ œ ï› and no ‹á›. The acute marks *Culombálu* as
  a foreign name carried into Merch/Borlish unaltered, the way *Waronøu* and *Cráyenschot* are.

### Deganny is not a random peg

`Deganny` [Deganwy] recurs, and always as a British maritime/underworld node:

- the ***Glamour of Deganny***, the source that records the mid-C19 British underworld slang from which
  Borlish borrows **vlangr** "fence" — a word whose chain is **Suahile *mlanguri* "healer" → Ongutian
  [Zanzibari] argot "profiteer, black marketeer" → British underworld slang → Borlish**
  (`dictionary/216.2_vlangr_fence.md`)
- "New Year's in Deganny is a harrowing experience" (`dictionary/101.3`)
- "listeners **from Deganny to Baldran**" — a merism meaning "everywhere" (`pre-2022:7170`)

> **Note.** ***Baldran*** appears **exactly once**, in that merism, and is defined nowhere. It is an
> unexplained far-pole toponym in a Welsh-facing text. It is available, and it would sit very naturally
> as the African end of a British sea route whose home end is Deganny.

### Options for what *Culombálu* is

House style for names is well established — a local word, often a category error, hardened into a proper
noun through a chain of languages (*Mendeva* < Vask *mendeval* "west"; *Cappatia* < Run'allo *kay pacha*
"this world"; *Angelica* < "bone country"; *Brasil* < a myth). Applying it:

1. **A local etymon misconstrued** — the house default. A Bantu-shaped word (the phonotactics
   Cu-lom-bá-lu invite Kikongo/Kimbundu/Umbundu) meaning something that is not a fleet at all — a
   landing, an ancestor, a season, an obligation — taken by Borlish factors for the name of the concern.
2. **A dynastic or corporate honorific**, the *Ambrosia* pattern — named for a person or house.
3. **Romance folk-etymology** — reshaped toward Latin *columba* "dove", giving a false transparency
   ("the Dove fleet") that the etymological dictionaries have to correct. This is the most Boralverse of
   the three, because it lets a later reference work be *wrong* in an interesting way.

Whichever is chosen, the fleet name gives you the polity name for free, or vice versa.

## 4. African material already inside Borlish

Thin, but it establishes the routes by which African words legitimately arrive.

| Word | Sense | Route |
|---|---|---|
| **gorray** /goˈre/ | kola nut | "via continental Romance from one of the **Bamackon languages** of West Africa (cf. Wolof *guru*)" (`pre-2022:7843`) |
| **mause** | banana, plantain | First attested in Boral **1397 N**; "borrowed somehow through the European trade mesh (some suggest a British intermediary but it is impossible…)" — and *Bamackon mause* is named as the distant luxury reaching the Middlesea |
| **dalassine** | cinnamon | C16, from **Suahile *dalasini*** via British (or less plausibly Vascon) merchants "who picked up the term in Ungotia" (`pre-2022:7609`) |
| **vlangr** | fence, receiver of stolen goods | mid-C19, **Suahile → Ongutian argot → British underworld slang → Borlish** (`dictionary/216.2`) |

**Two of the four run through British merchants.** Both West African words are Bamackon-mediated. There
is no attested loan from anywhere on the Atlantic coast between the Senegal and the Cape — the exact
stretch the staddomains occupy.

## 5. The onomastic sweep — diaspora surnames

I extracted all 392 person-entities from `all_claims_final.yaml` and checked every name whose morphology
fits no European, Arabic, Mendevan or South Asian pattern already established in canon.

### Ruled out (with reasons — worth recording so they aren't chased again)

- **Milda'h Ragner**, **Thoffina'h Girmont** — the ‹'h› is a **Welsh patronymic**. The works index
  (`Works of Writing/- Works by Author Surname.md`) files them as "**ver** Ragner, Milda" and "**ver**
  Girmont, Thoffina", i.e. *ferch*. Cambric, not diaspora.
- **Adamara Godwilham** — *Tell Me, Telmy!* (1936); her father is **Bartelmy**, and "Godwilham Desk" is a
  Kentish media pseudonym of the Borlish author Willaum Desq. A Merch/Kentish surname.
- **Mollam Bushta**, **Poshack Nerwaman**, **Tazim Gennun**, **Sólin Izcoál**, **Yollamaniza Elcabir** — all
  explicitly **Mashick**. **Bayac Cosωteg** — **Hasiny** (the ‹ω› is a Jaughta orthographic convention).
  **Cai Sonquo** — New Leudong. **Indvar Vijarrania** — Ajamarn.

### Live candidates

| Name | Attestation | Why it is a candidate |
|---|---|---|
| **Fuyo Jaðom** | ● fl. 1980, **Borlish** author of *Neyanç a Sagnt Stevan* ("Saint Stephen's Snowfall"), short stories adapted from tales he told his children (`Mapbook/Borland.md:214`) | Given name fits no European pattern. Surname is fully Borlish-orthographic (‹ð›) — i.e. **naturalised spelling on a non-native stem**, exactly what a third- or fourth-generation surname looks like. |
| **Endric Kirennaga** | ● fl. 2009, **Borlish** masquira author of *Apollon Cogmaðer* ("Apollo on Holiday") (`Mapbook/Borland.md:215`) | Merch/Germanic given name + a four-syllable surname with no European shape. The classic diaspora signature: local forename, retained surname. |
| **Takach Domoko** | Author of *We Will Burn Together*, "notable for its openly tovarick characters" (`Masquira romance.md:29`) | **Nationality is never stated anywhere.** Wholly unclaimed, and the most African-looking name in the corpus. |

> **This is the single most useful finding for the entry.** Borland already has two modern authors — one
> from 1980, one from 2009 — whose names canon has never explained. A C17–C18 Borlish-guild staddomain in
> Atlantic Africa, with C19–C20 migration back along the trade route to Damvath and the east-coast cities,
> **retro-explains both without contradicting a single line.** Borland has the precedent: it took
> "substantial Welsh, Devoner and Kernish immigration" during the Wars of Fealty, and Danish immigration
> into "Damvath and other east-coast cities" gave it the word *toscivr* "sandwich".

## 6. The modern era, and the City Wars hook

Canon on the 1940s war is **one sentence and one inference**, which is an opportunity rather than a problem.

- The **Anvers Showburg** "had been a casualty of the **City Wars**, burning down in a collateral fire in
  **1948**" (`notes/225.5`). **This is the only occurrence of the phrase "City Wars" in the corpus.**
- **Japetos III**, the Gadire–Paratzon submarine cable, was "originally conceived by the two
  administrations in concert in **1943**", then "delayed several times due both to budgetary concerns and,
  of course, **the outbreak of war**", conveying its first messages in **1950** (`excerpt/115.1`,
  `Mapbook/Morrack.md`).

So: a war that broke out **after 1943**, was still burning cities in **1948**, and delayed a
**Morrack–Ambrosia Atlantic cable** by seven years. The 1937 **Veldsvindung** (global economic collapse
and "pursuant general poverty") precedes it; the Drengot Collusion survives it and falls in **1961**.

> **Why this is the right war for this entry.** The one concrete thing canon tells us the City Wars did
> to the wider world is **interrupt Atlantic shipping between Africa and the Novomund**. A polity whose
> identity is a merchant fleet on exactly that run is the natural witness. And an Atlantic African
> staddomain-descended polity in the 1940s sits at the intersection of every fact already fixed:
> Morrack's ports at one end, Ambrosia at the other, a British coastal chain behind it, and a Borlish
> guild charter underneath it.

### Also available for the modern layer

- **Libya** ● is "a significant refiner and exporter of **cuproses**" today — the one modern African
  industrial fact in canon.
- The **LGT** has grown "from a dozen entrants to over sixty" with a bilateral committee arbitrating
  eligibility and **population bounds** (`notes/207.5`). No African entrant is named. A staddomain-scale
  polity arguing about the population floor is a very cheap, very in-register modern detail.
- **Poshack Nerwaman** (1814–1893), "the most celebrated lady explorer of her generation", travelled
  Cappatia in the 1840s–50s and wrote accounts "part natural history, part ethnography, part unrepentant
  philosophical musings" that "stayed in print across the Vetomund well into the twentieth century"
  (`notes/229.5`). Her *African* travels are unwritten. If you ever want a C19 European-facing account of
  this coast, she already exists, is already famous, and is already the right kind of unreliable.

## 7. Decisions the entry will have to make

Listed so they can be made deliberately rather than by accident.

1. **Name the polity** (and settle whether *Culombálu* is the polity, the fleet, the port or the house).
2. **Where on the coast.** The corpus fixes Sant Adrian [Bioko] as Vascon and Bamackon as the C14 power
   to the north. The Gulf of Guinea and the Congo mouth are both entirely free.
3. **What the staddomain traded.** Canon gives you kola and bananas northward already; Potchware, the
   Cappatian staddomain analogue, is "known for its **sugar and cocoa** exports" (`all_claims:11290`).
4. **Slavery.** The corpus has never addressed it — `\bslave` returns five files, all etymological. An
   Atlantic staddomain founded by *trade guilds* in the C17–C18 is the point where the setting either
   answers this question or visibly declines to. Worth deciding before drafting rather than during.
5. **How much of the City Wars to fix.** The entry can gesture (a witness's memory, a cancelled sailing,
   a requisitioned hull) without settling belligerents.
6. **Watch the overlap with 236.5.** "Sold as Seen" was already a repatriation-adjacent piece about
   removed artefacts. A Culombálu ship returning from a Welsh display in 1995–96 is thematically close.
   Either lean in deliberately or steer to a different beat.

## 8. Format proposal — no apparatus

The last two entries (235.5, 236.5) both hang on a `===` block with numbered footnotes. Two candidates
that carry none:

### Primary: a recorded interview (*entrcollocq*)

An oral-history recording for a maritime or diaspora archive, transcribed — a person in their eighties
or nineties who worked the Culombálu boats, or whose family came up the route into Borland, remembering
the fleet, the war years, and the ship going to Wales.

- **Structurally footnote-free.** The apparatus is the interviewer's questions and bracketed stage
  directions (*[laughs]*, *[indicating]*, *[recording resumes]*) — an editorial voice inside the text
  rather than beneath it.
- **The word already exists**: **entrcollocq** "interview, a conversation between a journalist and their
  subject" (94–97.5 Lexember set), and **sobot** [radio] dates from 1903 in Hasiny.
- **It solves the invention problem.** A witness is *allowed* to be vague about geopolitics, wrong about
  dates, and partial about the war. You can establish a whole polity through what one person took for
  granted, without having to draw the map. For a region this blank, that is the cheapest possible entry
  at the highest possible resolution.
- **It is a register the blog has never used**: spoken, digressive, self-interrupting, in the first
  person and in the present.
- It braids everything above naturally — the fleet, the guild charter, the diaspora surname, the City
  Wars, the Deganny display, the modern polity.

### Alternative: a sailing notice and freight tariff

A season's advertised sailings with rates by commodity and class, posted by the fleet's agents.

- Tabular, cold, and entirely without apparatus.
- Shows the trade system rather than describing it — ports, goods, currencies, who is allowed to travel
  in which class, what is carried and what is not.
- **Weaker on the modern/City Wars requirement**: a tariff is a snapshot and cannot remember anything.
  Best as a companion piece to the interview in a later post, or as the `.1` excerpt to a future entry.
