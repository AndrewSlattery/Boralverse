continued excerpt from The Old Country, a tourist guidebook about Britain aimed at travellers from British Mendeva written by Ossewma'h Ariandro.

…founding a kingdom. They believed they were forming a fleet. A coast that lives by one trade needs one navy, one court of appeal for its contracts and one person able to sign for all of it, and the lords of the tallaths had spent a generation learning that they could not afford to argue over who that person was.

The Compact therefore gave the crown their navies, their coinage and their dealings with foreign powers, and nothing else. The tallaths kept their own courts, levies, taxes and languages, and the crown has never acquired the power to tax them directly. This is why a Kernish judgment cannot be appealed in Cartaw [Cardiff], why the Devon customs schedule is a separate document from the British one, and why a Briton asked who governs Britain will usually ask what you mean by govern.

The king who took the crown is not much remembered, but the date is. The Compact was sworn on David's Day, when every lord of every tallath was already holding a feast, and so the New Year, the patron's day and the founding of the kingdom have been one festival ever since. This goes some way to explaining Deganny.

The war they left early

The Wars of Fealty began over the Redstone Chair, a throne to which Britain had no claim and for which it had no use. Britain joined nonetheless, from a position of strength, and spent the early years extracting concessions from Markland. At Rexam in 1483 the last Duke of Chester was killed, and when the attack on Chester itself was rebuffed shortly afterwards, Britain—with the border where it wanted it—simply stopped. The rest of Albion fought on for another thirty years.

Britons call this statecraft; their neighbours call it the national character. When the Second Great Dying arrived in 1519 it found the island exhausted, and Markland and English Kent buried almost twice as many per head as Norman Kent across the channel.

The marriages of the period are easy to miss, since few were British on both sides. Margaret of Iscombe [Exeter] married Prince Mark of Tolose, heir to Vascony, so that Ambrose the Ascendant and his elder sister Alexandra had a Devoner mother. Alexandra's second marriage, made during the wars but before her famous voyage, was into Wales; Dormaget's 1934 film The Ascendant, which cuts between the courts at Huzatre [Bayonne] and Cartaw, is clearer on this than most histories.

Howel's road

King Howel spent the peace on harbours. By the turn of the century a chain of British victualling stations ran down the Atlantic coast of Africa: not colonies, but a road, built to reach Asia directly by sea and to break the Borlish hold on the northern trade.

The Phœbos under Martan mac Kellot went down that road and kept going. It rounded Africa to Ungotia [Zanzibar], where its crew picked up the word that Borlish still uses for cinnamon, and was guided by Umanick [Omani] and then Chinese pilots to Gurjat [Gujarat], where it spent several months of 1512, and on through Madray [Madurai]. In 1516 it reached Borune [Borneo], and at Sinquan [Singkawang] the following year the Morrack [Moroccan] ship Cynthia found it at anchor.

The popular story that the two crews could not understand each other is wrong; a dozen men aboard the Phœbos had some Arabic. What actually passed between the ships is unknown, because the relevant pages of al-Casmi's otherwise immaculate logbook are missing. In the 1927 film One Day in Borune, mac Kellot's first mate hails the Cynthia with O Gastor 'ila Bolux! "From Castor to Pollux!" There is no evidence for this, and everyone prefers it anyway.

Mac Kellot, having been told how much ocean was left, declined to sail round the world. Word that Morrack and Britain meant to girdle the globe nonetheless reached the Southern Emperor, and in 1520 his Hangthen Edict declared that China would tie the ropes herself.
