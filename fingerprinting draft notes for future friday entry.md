###.5: Hands Given and Hands Left

    excerpt in translation from The Registered Hand: Identification from the Leu Ledgers to the Grimmer, a history by New Leudong historian Mared Hau, originally published in New Leudong Cambrick in 2009 through the University of Mehannin.

…two different things have been called by the one name. A fingerprint, in the strict sense used throughout this book, is an impression of the ridges of the fingertip given on purpose: pressed in ink on a contract, a muster roll or a register card by someone who knows it is being taken. A fingermark is the trace a hand leaves without meaning to, in sweat, grease, soot or blood, on whatever it touches. The first answers the question "who is this?", and the second "who was here?". They have separate histories. The fingerprint was a Cathayan institution for some three centuries before Europe made systematic use of it. The fingermark was first read in Europe, in workshops that had no register to search. The two were not brought together until the Long Peace, and a fingermark could not be searched against a whole register, with no suspect in mind, until the grimmers of the late twentieth century.

Before the registers

Ridges show on anything soft or dirty enough to take them, and the hand has always served as a mark. The manuals of the Qin magistrates already record handprints at the scene of a burglary. In the first millennium, contracts, bonds and deeds of divorce in the Chinas were marked by parties who had no seal, who laid a finger on the paper and ruled a line across at each joint. The seal was the mark of rank, and the finger the mark of everyone else. An older folk practice counted the whorls on a person's fingers to tell their fortune, and the Cathayan names for the two commonest patterns, the bushel (a whorl) and the basket (a loop), come from its rhymes.

Europe had seals and signatures, and those who could not write made crosses or imitations of writing: about one in eight of the Roman lead tablets from the spring at Axbane carries only rows of marks imitating cursive, made by petitioners who could not write. Seals themselves came to Borland under an Arabic name; Borlish catoum "seal" arrived in the fourteenth century by way of Spain.

Neither the Chinas nor Europe needed, in the first millennium, to identify strangers in large numbers. People who had to be recognised again were marked on the body: the Romans branded runaway slaves, and the old Chinese codes tattooed the faces of convicts. Identification by the hand became necessary only where a state governed people it could not tell apart, and could not or would not mark them.

The Leu ledgers

The first such state was Leu Cathay. The Kitan had always governed in two ways at once: their own people by clan and kin, and the settled farmers of the north by household registers kept on the old Chinese model. When the Leu conquered the divided south piecemeal through the thirteenth century, they took on millions of new subjects in a country their officials did not know, speaking languages few of those officials spoke. Southern households traded names and registrations among themselves to escape tax and labour service, and a Kitan clerk could not tell a man from his cousin.

The remedy was the finger. From the 1250s the head of every registered southern household was made to press an inked finger beside his entry in the county ledger, and to press it again whenever he paid tax or reported for service. Men conscripted to labour on canals and walls were finger-marked on the muster rolls, and convicts on the prison rolls. The Leu court was devout, and had abolished the tattooing of convicts' faces on the Buddhist ground that the body should not be marked. The officers who monitored the effects of government policy, an institution older than the dynasty, were asked whether reoffending had risen since the tattoos were abolished, and could not answer until the prison rolls carried fingerprints too. Their reports are the earliest surviving attempt anywhere to count reoffenders, and the first documents to treat a finger as a record of a person rather than a mark of assent.

The ledgers worked within a county. A clerk could turn to a man's page and compare the finger before him with the finger in the book, and false names became harder to sustain. At any larger scale they were useless. A ledger was ordered by village and household, and a stranger who gave a false name and a false village could not be found in it, because there was no way to begin from the finger and arrive at the page. The ledgers were books of witnesses, not indexes.

They were also hated. When the Leu collapsed and the south consolidated under the Southern Emperors, the county ledgers were burned along with the Kitan tax rolls, and the Southern Empire has never since taken the hands of its own subjects. In the south "a hand in the book" still means servitude. Southern administration returned to seals and to the mutual guarantee of households, each answering for its neighbours. Private contracts continued to be finger-marked as they always had been. It was the state's ledger that the south rejected, not the mark.

The Cathayan register

After the final sundering in the late fourteenth century, Cathay was a smaller state with a long frontier against a neighbour that shared its script and much of its culture. Spies, deserters and smugglers could pass for either side. The north had not burned its ledgers, and the habit survived. Through the fifteenth century the frontier garrisons finger-marked their muster rolls, and from the 1480s anyone crossing the frontier carried a pass bearing a fingerprint, which was pressed again and compared at the far post. Like the Leu ledgers, these records could confirm who a man was but could not find him.

The problem was solved in the middle of the sixteenth century by surveyors. Cathayan surveying was precise enough that its surveyor's span would one day fix half of the dime foot, and in the 1540s the Board of Lands lent several surveyors to the frontier registry to bring order to its records. Their method is credited to one of them, Lou Hinzun, whose manual of 1553 survives in a later copy. Lou classified the hand rather than the person. He first recorded which of the ten fingers bore a bushel. There are thirty-two ways for the five fingers of one hand to bear bushels or not, and so 1,024 for the pair, and each had its own drawer. Within a drawer the cards were ordered by counting ridges. On every finger that bore a basket, the clerk drew a line from its centre, the eye, to the small triangle of ridges beside it, the fork, and counted the ridges the line crossed, as a surveyor counts the contours crossed by his chain.

The written form of the classification relied on Cathay's soundwise use of Sinic runes. Lou gave each of the thirty-two patterns of one hand a rune read for its sound alone, so that a pair of hands could be written, and spoken, as two syllables, followed by shorter syllables for the ridge-counts. This hand-name told a clerk which drawer to open and where in the drawer to look. Because it could be read aloud, it could also be called across a registry, sent by courier, or copied into a letter by someone who had never seen the card. Every registered Cathayan has had a hand-name alongside the name given at birth since at least the eighteenth century, and the hand-names of famous people are widely known.

By 1600 the register had been extended from the frontier to the whole of Cathay: soldiers, officials, convicts, licensed merchants and boatmen, and anyone who drew a grain dole. It was not a criminal register. Most of the hands in it belonged to people who had never been accused of anything, and in Cathay, as later in New Leudong, giving one's fingerprint carried no more stigma than giving one's name.

Its claims rested on doctrine and experience rather than proof. Cathayan Buddhists taught that the ridges are written by a person's past deeds, which no two people share, and the registry could point out that in fifty years no two of its cards had been found to agree. Nor did a match convict anyone. Cathayan law required a confession. A match in the register justified holding and questioning a suspect, or proved that he had been convicted before and should be punished as a reoffender; it did not prove what he had done. The register was a means of recognising people, and it was never searched for a hand that had left a mark at the scene of a crime. So far as the records show, nobody in Cathay thought of trying.

The register had its own crimes. A card could be swapped, or a clerk bribed to file it under the wrong hand-name, and the commonest offence of the Cathayan registry clerk was selling a clean card to a man with a record. The registry answered by keeping its cards in duplicate, in two cities. The Cathayan tapsue or "magpie" tales, which long predate the masquira romance of Europe and are often compared with its kevler stories, grew up around these offences, and their heroes steal register cards as often as jewels.

The clerks worked with the naked eye, or with lenses of rock crystal borrowed from the seal-carvers, which were small, costly and few. Counting ridges was slow work, and the clerk's squint was a recognised complaint. That changed only in the seventeenth century, when clear glass lenses reached Cathay from the west along the Gurjati trade mesh, and a count that had taken a morning could be made in minutes.

Clear glass

Europe's first lenses were also stones. The Norman word behind Borlish caglou "lens" meant a small stone, and the earliest magnifiers in Europe were polished pebbles of rock crystal or beryl, rare enough to be left in wills. Glass was no use for the purpose. The glass of medieval Europe was the green jackdaw glass of Andalus, made there since the ninth century and exported across the continent, and it could not be seen through clearly at any thickness.

The glasshouses of Bavarn [Bavaria] changed this in the fifteenth century. Their masters found ways to make a glass that was nearly colourless, and by the 1480s clear panes from Bavarn were being fitted in the great houses of Albion beside the older green. Clear glass could be ground into a lens as easily as a pebble, and far more cheaply. By the middle of the sixteenth century reading lenses were common among scholars, engravers, jewellers and clerks, and the lens-grinders of Augsburg had a guild of their own. By about 1700 sailors carried collapsible spyglasses.

Clear glass also did something its makers had not intended: it made surfaces on which a fingermark could be seen. A green bottle shows little, but a clear pane, a mirror or a polished lens shows every touch.

The mark left behind

The first people to read fingermarks deliberately were glassworkers. The rule of the lens-grinder's trade, then as now, was to handle a finished glass only by its rim, and the inspectors of the Bavarn glasshouses and the Augsburg workshops learned to tell from the marks on a rejected pane or lens which journeyman had broken the rule. The guild books record fines levied on this evidence from the 1580s. The inspectors compared the marks with the hands of the dozen or so men who could have made them, using the lenses they ground themselves. They had no register and needed none, because the suspects were always a small group known in advance.

The earliest theft on record to be solved in this way took place in an Augsburg workshop in 1611, when a set of finished lenses went missing and the marks on the empty case were matched to an apprentice. For most of the seventeenth century the method stayed where it had begun, in closed communities with few possible culprits: workshops, colleges, monasteries and great households, where a steward with a lens could settle which servant had been in a locked cupboard. It reached print in 1658, when an Augsburg physician described the ridges of the fingertip as seen through a lens, the pores along their crests, and the sweat by which a fingertip leaves its pattern on glass. He did not claim that every hand was different, only that he had never failed to tell two people's marks apart.

Westward

The fingerprint itself reached the west by two routes, which carried two different versions of it.

The Southern Empire, having refused to take the hands of its own subjects, took the hands of foreigners. A Chinese subject could be vouched for by his household and his neighbours; a foreigner had no one to answer for him, and his fingerprint stood in for a guarantor. The Chinese quarter of Sinquan, on Borune, recorded every foreign sailor who landed for its annual fair with a fingerprint beside his name. The harbour rolls of the Great Borunesk Meeting of 1517, now scattered among universities across the globe, include the fingerprints of the crews of the Phœbos and the Cynthia. They are the oldest surviving fingerprints of Europeans, and among them are the hands of the two men of Tecamaw who sailed on the Cynthia, whose names are first recorded in Sinquan's annals. The crew of the Morrack ship Orion, who met Chinese merchants at Mombasa in 1468, may have been asked for their fingerprints half a century earlier, but no document from that meeting survives.

The Cathayan version travelled with trade. When the trade mesh of Gurjat reached Cathay in the seventeenth century, the Gurjati merchants there were registered like everyone else. They took the practice home for their own guild rolls and debt bonds, where a hand-name was harder to forge than a signature, and carried it along their mesh from Ungotia and Vascon Sant Adrian in the west to Cathay in the east. When the merchants of Moscat were expelled in 1652, many of them Gurjati, their fingerprinted bonds were scattered through the ports of Arabia and the Middlesea. Bavarn lenses travelled east along the same routes.

New Leudong, formed by Cathayan state-building alongside British settlement from the late seventeenth century, took the Cathayan register with it and registered everyone from the start, British settlers included. It was the first polity outside Cathay in which every adult had a card, and the card belonged to the individual rather than to a household or a parish. Historians have connected this with New Leudong's early embrace of the Household Renovation two centuries later: a person whose identity is their own card can belong to any household, or to none, without troubling the authorities.

In the Democratic Wars of the mid-eighteenth century, China was embroiled in the contest for India. Through one of its protectorates it attacked the polities of eastern India, and it took the hands of the people it conquered there, as it took the hands of foreigners everywhere. British trading interests in Bengal had to pay hired soldiers and pensioners whom they could not tell apart, and within a decade they had copied the method from the protectorate's clerks. In Bengal, as in the Southern Empire, the fingerprint was the mark of a subject people, and British opinion carried that association home.

The end of the Democratic Wars set great numbers of people moving, and pennackit dysentery moved with them, spreading worldwide from 1753. Quarantine stations had to know that a traveller who claimed to have served his days had actually done so, and passes bearing a fingerprint, pressed on arrival and compared on release, spread from the Indian ports to the Middlesea and the Atlantic within a generation. For many Europeans the quarantine pass was the first fingerprint they ever gave.

Damvath

The two traditions met in Damvath during the Long Peace. The city was growing fast with immigrants from Denmark and Willemy, and its tuppence scouts, an early detective force, faced the problem Cathay had faced in the thirteenth century: a thief who gave a false name could not be recognised when he was taken again. In the 1780s the scouts began to keep cards of the hands of those they arrested, filed in Lou's 1,024 drawers from descriptions of the Cathayan register that had reached Borland with the Indian trade. From the city's glaziers and lens-grinders they also knew that a hand could be read on glass.

The first conviction in Borland on the evidence of a fingermark came in 1797, against a thief who had forced a glazed cabinet in the Jacobin Estates and whose marks on the glass were found among the scouts' cards. It was also the first time anywhere that a mark left at a scene was found in a register. A single mark cannot be filed in Lou's drawers, which need all ten fingers, and the scouts found it only because their register was still small enough to search card by card. As registers grew this became impossible, and for almost two centuries afterwards a fingermark could be compared only with the hands of known suspects.

The Damvath underworld adapted quickly. The Enumeration of Thieves' Cant (1793) already has words for the gloves worn on a job and for the scouts' book of hands. Borland's courts admitted the scouts' evidence as they admitted a physician's, as the opinion of men practised in something the jury was not.

Doubts

Learned opinion in Europe was slower. The Cathayan doctrine that no two hands are alike arrived together with the fortune-telling rhymes about bushels and baskets, and to many European scholars it looked like a branch of palmistry. The Deviance movement's enthusiasm for folk magic did nothing for its reputation.

A more serious objection came from life theory. Rachel Desselut had argued in 1743 that living matter is built according to a Method it carries within itself, and her followers reasoned that twins alike in every visible feature, and so presumably carrying the same Method, should carry the same ridges. If they did, the Cathayan claim was false. The question was settled in 1811 by Manesh Camdar of Gurjat, who searched a century of Gurjati guild rolls, and the copies of Cathayan cards kept with them, for twins, and found no pair whose hands agreed. He concluded that the ridges are formed in the womb under the guidance of the Method but are not dictated by it, a conclusion that mattered more to life theory than to the scouts.

Sending a fingerprint at a distance raised a different problem. The Steeplepost Tapestry, completed at Aiga d'Angoixa in Ambrosia in 1820, could display a picture sent along the steeplemesh, and the Ambrosian police experimented with sending fingerprints on it. After the Lies of 1824, when forged images on the Tapestry reported the governor's death and a declaration of war from New Provence, no registry would act on a transmitted picture. Registries sent the classification instead. Lou's hand-name, rewritten in letters and numbers, became one of the first steeplescripts used by police, and it remained the usual form in which registries exchanged fingerprints until the late twentieth century.

The Modest registers

The reputation of the fingerprint in Europe was fixed less by the scouts than by the Modest Arrangement. In its later years in Vascony, under King Hernand and the Vallabrì administration, the morality police took the hands of Deviants, of tovaric men and women, and of women working in brothels, and kept them in registers modelled on those of Damvath. The resurgence of the Arrangement in Gaul and Spain in the early nineteenth century spread the practice, and its decline spread the reaction. When the Kingdom of the Danaw collapsed after the Second German War of the 1820s, several of the Riverine Cities burned the royal registers in their streets, and Belgrade, with its semi-public tovaric community, resolved to keep no register of persons at all.

The Laic Declarations of 1850 turned this into a dilemma. Uncoupling religious from administrative authority took the parish register away from the state as its record of who people were, and the obvious replacement was a civil register on the New Leudong model, in which everyone gave a fingerprint and no one was marked by giving it. The laic polities divided. Some adopted civil registers within a generation. Others, the Riverine Cities first among them, would keep no register of hands outside their prisons.

The masquira romance, which began in 1860 with Ezio Carvallo's In Either a Mask or a Suit, grew out of the same unease. Its heroes live under hidden identities in a world that wants to record them, and the gloves worn with the mask became the emblem of the genre. They were also a piece of realism. Every kevler thief of the Good Game period wears gloves, because by then every reader knew why.

Machines and their limits

In the Good Game period the registries outgrew what clerks could search by hand. The first devices to be called indagours, in the 1880s, were sorting machines for register cards notched along one edge with the hand-name, which could pull every card in a given drawer out of a tray of thousands. The Drengot Collusion built a central registry for its member polities on this basis. When the Collusion established the first Ministry for Consequences in 1932, one of its first studies used the registry to count reoffenders, the question the Leu policy officers had asked seven centuries before.

Registries could also be lost. The registry at Anvers [Antwerp] burned with the city in the City Wars in 1948, and false identities were common in the region for a decade afterwards. They could also be abused. Among the clandestine activities of the Red House in Roun [Rouen] enumerated in the Darchild memorandum was the lifting of fingermarks from glasses, cups and door-handles at diplomatic receptions, and their filing in a register of people who had never been asked for a print. The Kentish ostracon reforms that followed the Furore of 1961 required that any register of hands kept by a state be open to inspection by the people in it.

The fingerprint and the fingermark did not finally become a single practice until the late twentieth century, when grimmers built at Mehannin in New Leudong could compare a single mark against every card in a register without a suspect in mind. The register it was first used on was New Leudong's own, by then the oldest continuous civil register of hands outside Cathay.

Today some polities register every citizen and others only those they imprison; the Southern Empire still takes the hands of no one but foreigners, and Belgrade still keeps no register of persons at all. Universal registration is a ready example of Ollamec Vamncoury's argument that Ubiquity and Vicinity are not cleanly opposed. Its supporters have argued for it on grounds of Ubiquity, that everyone should be treated alike, and on grounds of Vicinity, that a community has a right to know who belongs to it; its opponents have argued against it on both grounds as well.
