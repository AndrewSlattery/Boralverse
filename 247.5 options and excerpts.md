**Frame.** "Collected notes on Italy", on the 21.5 *Kent* / 244.5 model: plain section headings and a list of notable people. The alternative is *The Pocket Vetomund (cont.)*, with entries for ITALY, LOMBARDY, VENISE and SAINT MARINUS.

### Medieval Italy

**1.** Surrounded by three empires, Italy at the beginning of the twelfth century was a volatile place. Maritime republics vied with monarchies for control of the seas. The north consolidated its power and looked south for new lands to conquer, and for the greatest prize of all, the seat of all Christendom. In 1120 there were five powers:
- the Papal Dominium, ruled from Rome by Pope Damian V (1110–21), born Humbert of Ravenna in 1047;
- the Duchy of Tuscany, ruled from Florence by Duchess Matilda (1102–46) of the Pisan dynasty;
- the Kingdom of Lombardy, ruled from Pavia by King Jacob III (1087–1130) of the Verona branch of the Adelchians;
- the Republic of Jeans, led by Console Franco Cicala of the Cicale merchants (1119–22?);
- the Republic of Venise, at a momentary territorial maximum after taking Ancona and its Corfu holding in 1112, probably led by Parre Branco Magnossone (c. 1120–25 and 1136–42).

[**never posted**: *A Political Mapbook of Middle-Age Europe* caption, Scaunç daily notes 30 Jugn; Humbert of Ravenna also in 95.5]

**2.** Medieval Italy had more than one Lombard kingdom. In 987 Lombardy conquered the Kingdom of the North Lombards, whose capital was Pavia. The North Lombard ruling family fled to the Second Roman Empire and gained appreciable clout there. Some of them administered the empire's western themes, and the written accounts of others are history's strongest sources for certain events of the German Secession. From the tenth century into the Second Tetrarchy, Lombardy also contested Verona and its province with the German Empire.

[987 and Pavia: **never posted**; the exiles: 65.5, 127.1]

**3.** Jeans, the oldest of the so-called Latin Republics, came into existence in the tenth century. Together with Venise it is a counterpoint to the notion that the Second Tetrarchy consisted solely of great centralised empires. In 1207 Jeans took the Tyrrhene Isles of Corse and Sardine from Roman control, though only Corse remained in its orbit for long.

[131.5]

**4.** The Autonome University of Saint Marinus, one of the oldest still-running places of learning in the world, was founded in Lombardy in the eleventh century. Its scholars were among the first to translate mathematical texts from Arabic into Latin, and they compiled a *liber numerorum* containing the earliest-known enumeration of arrumb [cosine] values in Europe. By the twelfth century the dattilo [dactyl] lines had become characteristic of processional chant in the churches of Lombardy and Burgundy.

[54.5, 197.5, 230.5; see the 1216/1261 note above]

**5.** After the German Empire collapsed in the thirteenth century, Venise became Furlain's most frequent rival. Furlain suffered a severe earthquake in 1348, which destroyed both the castle and the cathedral of Odin [Udine]. Ablate [negative] numbers were already recording debts in the account-books of Venise and other trade cities before European mathematicians made them respectable in the late fifteenth century.

[145.5, 159.1, 123.5, 128.5]

### Revitalist Italy

**6.** Piracy was long the scourge of the Middlesea republics, and it thrived after the Novomundane Landfall of 1471, peaking at the turn of the sixteenth century as Novomundane wealth flooded the region. In the century before 1550, Rome and the other Italian states had mostly been playing catch-up in Classical mathematics and science. They lacked the Novomundane wealth that funded new inventors in Vascony, Kernow and Morrack, and their unchecked monarchs held them back further.

[15.1, 120.1; partly **never posted**]

**7.** The Great Fracture of the mid-sixteenth century severely diminished the Papacy's temporal power. On paper the Pope remained an absolute ruler of scattered provinces in Italy and Germany, but in practice the city of Rome came under the new Triunvirato. The Triunvirato lifted Rome's old prohibitions on blasphemous arts and philosophical publications and invited great minds from across Europe, and the first Brethin House in Italy was established. The result was the Revitalist period, whose best-known work is probably Lorenzo dell'Acchedia's sculpture *I Macchine di Helio*.

[95.5, 232.5, 59.1]

### Modern Italy

**8.** In the eighteenth century, popular unrest over bread and grain in Venise and Crovatia stood as a warning to neighbouring Bavarn. During the Long Peace, Lombard and Napolitan heredical theorists made notable advances in farming and husbandry.

[86.5; 3.1 and pre-post: Thaubrett of Crowmarsh's 1865 survey]

**9.** In the 1820s, after the Second German War, emigrants from Barcelon and Lombardy came in large numbers to Santrafew in New Provence. The Lombard marquisates, among them Cremuna, were abolished in the 1860s. During the Good Game period, the Lombard diplomat Audace Saviano was instrumental in forming the Italian Concert in 1891. By 1970 Lombardy was importing astraphor from as far away as Portingale.

[60.5, 210.5, 91.5, 55.5]

**10.** In the late 1960s and early 1970s, the General Italian Fervour produced a wave of "giftales": fiction about Italian places and people, or the Italian diaspora. Examples include Carlyn van Stede's *While Gold Yet Runs in My Veins* (1965) and Carla fi Lohis's notoriously inaccurate *A Single Misstep* (1966).

[26.5, 30.5, 45.5; titles pre-post]

### Languages

**11.** Italy's languages include the Italian of Tuscany, Venise Italian (Venisian), Furlain Italian, Jean, Napolitan and Lombard. Tuscan's prestige and trade wealth lent many words to other European languages; Borlish has *broccat* "brocade" and *sbolognar* "to foist upon". From Lombard, Borlish took *zizania* "discord" in the seventeenth century, and *bonaventur* "happenstance" came via French from Lombard *bunaventura* "good fortune". Merch *ablate* "negative" comes from Lombard *ablad*, first used in financial accounts. During the Good Game period, Venise Italian borrowed *rasgatto* "resolution of a mystery" from the title of Olexi Harchenko's mystery trevold, and the word went on into many European languages.

[dictionary entries; 104.5, 128.5]

## B. The Middlesea and the Single Caliphate

**Why.** The Caliphate has appeared only as one of the four empires in 89.5, and the Middlesea has had only the mealbook (10.5). This option can also cash an old promissory note. A pre-post quiz asks, of Musab as-Sakr of Ispella, "during what activity did he fall to his death, aged 40?", and canon has never answered it, so the post would need your answer. **Downside:** it overlaps A on the republics and the piracy, so pick one or the other.

**1.** The Single Caliphate, ruled from Cairo, stretched at its height from Spain to Persia. It was the product of several centralising reforms in the early eleventh century, by which Cairo consolidated its authority over the successor states of the Abbasid Caliphate, though its more distant territories, such as Cordova and the Turkish lands, were never as closely ruled. Its provinces were admiralties. Ifricia, the later Libya, was ruled from Tunis, and its port of Trapoles was a major Middlesea port. [89.5, 28.5]

**2.** The Admiralty of Cordova, shortly after the Aragonese conquest of Toledo in 1198, was ruled by Abdul Raman IV (crowned 1188), with his court at Cordova and other centres at Seville, Murcia and Malaga. [**never posted**: mapbook caption; 39.5]

**3.** Nadim of Trapoles, a twelfth-century mathematician at the Observatory in Trapoles, formulated the Trapolitan identity, which for centuries made long multiplications easier. [46.5, 54.5]

**4.** In the last decade of the twelfth century, Musab as-Sakr of Ispella led an early faction for independence from the Caliphate. His death in a fall at the age of forty postponed such ideas for another two generations. The Brethin nun Isida of Toled (fl. 1220) lived during "the twilight of the Single Caliphate's Cairene authority over Andalus". In 1237 (634 AH) an independent admiralty was declared in Libya, at the same time as the Morrove admiralty to the west. [Musab: **never posted**, pre-post quiz; Isida: 214.5; 1237: 28.5, 63.1]

**5.** Between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries, several wars of conquest were waged in the Middlesea far from home, under varying degrees of Papal authority; Borlish calls them *mordeth*, from Welsh *mordeith* "sea voyage, raid". In the thirteenth century Provence became the direct link between the Burgundian capital at Geneva and the whole Middlesea sea-trade, and was known as *lo panier de Borgogne*, "the Burgundian breadbasket". [*mordeth*: 43.3, 5.5, 151.5; breadbasket: 141.5]

**6.** In the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, salt for curing flowed north and fish flowed south: Dunclothe sold its North Atlantic cod as far as the eastern Middlesea, and Vascony founded the New Navarre Enterprise in 1458 to secure its place in that trade. [14.5, 238.5]

**7.** After 1471 piracy thrived. In Marsella the raids became so frequent that many of the wealthy fled inland, and in the early sixteenth century Barcelon funded raids on the cities of newly independent Provence. In the seventeenth century Libya warred intermittently with Morrack over the trade into the eastern Middlesea, and the Bordew-Audaz canal, completed in 1679, joined the Atlantic to the Middlesea without passing through Morrack-held seas. [43.5, 141.5, 28.5, 67.5, 109.1]

**8.** Rebound is a wall-and-ball game played all around the coast. Any city or town worthy of the name has a rebound wall, though there is no unified ruleset. [238.1]

## C. Ireland

**Why.** Ireland has never had a .5, and it is a known canon blank for c. 1030–1820 (Canon Survey §8 #10). The 1028 mapbook caption has never been posted. **Downsides:** the vault page runs only about 500 words, so a full post would need new invented material for the middle centuries; it would also be a third Northwestern Isles .5 in a row, and the 1028 caption shares its year and frame with last week's Rodulf entry.

**1.** Ireland's Iron Age began, as on the other Northwestern Isles, around the ninth century aN. Its people were among the Insular Kelts, whom Ptolemy appears to have described as a single group, later divided by the English Strait and then by the Irish Sea. The Roman Empire, which conquered all of Borland and part of Albion, never attempted Ireland. Ireland's early monastic communities were comparatively insular, unlike the outward-looking monasteries of Borland. [164.5, 26.5, 120.5; Ptolemy and the Romans: **never posted**]

**2.** Danes settled in Ireland during the Norrish ascendancy of the eighth and ninth centuries, and Dublin became a Dane kingdom. Traffic between Dublin and Yorwick may have funded the rise of Wynneth. As the Dane Supremacy waned, Dublin joined a confederation of Dane kingdoms in which Northumber was first among equals. [3.5, 237.5, 154.5]

**3.** By 1028 the ever-fractious kingdoms of Ireland had lately been reduced to four by the breaking of the Ultaugh in the north-east: Tyrconnal, Connaught, Lenster and Munster. The tales of Tyrconnal and Lenster are dominated by wars at once with the Norrish to the east and the other Irish to the west, while the houses of Connaught and Munster vied for the position of High King of Ireland. [**never posted**: mapbook caption]

**4.** In the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, the fish and salt trade gave the nobility of the eastern Irish coast, with those of Wales, Dunclothe, Sodrick, Kernow and Devon, the habit of thinking of themselves as having common political interests. Parts of Ireland became an outlying tallath of the Kingdom of Britain, today its most populous. [238.5, 237.5]

**5.** In 1824 the assayer Yorreth ap Trahaen isolated an unknown mineral from the mines of Killdara. A Dublin pottery maker at first monopolised it for yellow-green glazes. Killdark, named via Welsh *kildárc* "from Killdara", is now the corporal in the vast majority of threshold mills. The Irish myth of Oil Breasail, a misty land to the west, almost certainly inspired the name of Brasil. [239.5, 71.5, 126.5; 35.1, 53.5]

## D. The Pocket Vetomund (cont.)

**Why.** It keeps last week's frame and fills it with four pages that have never had a .5 of their own. **Downsides:** it repeats the frame two weeks running, and most of the content restates posts (Desarden appears in 78.5, 82.5, 90.1 and 101.5; the New World Company in 14.5, 18.5 and 56.5).

**BELGRADE.** A city on the Danaw that gave its name to the Decadence of Belgrade, the last decades of the Kingdom of the Danaw, when the kingdom was at its height in territory and foreign relations even as its economy and stability declined. Afterwards it was one of the Riverine Cities, with a renowned Promethean club and a semi-public tovaric community. In 1850 it hosted the committee that produced the Laic Declarations. Their centenary was opened on 2 December 1950 with a keynote by Maya Tavares, and for the 1970 anniversary the Long Future project had academies publish dozens of popular works on the Declarations at the same time. [22.5, 115.5, 231.5, 220.5, 85.5]

**KINGDOM OF THE FRANKS.** Around 720 the Catumagin Accords fixed the border with the Kernish kingdoms along the Vire. In the ninth century the Francian Empire split. The eastern half joined Willemy to form the German Empire, and the western fragment, West Francia, ceded its Dane-settled north as Normandy in the tenth. A mapbook of the mid-tenth century shows Robert I (crowned 923) holding court in Paris, with Reims, Strasbourg, Loyons, Genevre, Roun and Amiens among its towns, and the Kingdom of Aquitain ruled from Bordew next door. [8.5, 18.5, 21.5; Robert I **never posted**]

**NACKON DAY.** An Asian kingdom whose monarchy survived the Democratic Wars with its powers trammelled. In 1887 the London ambassador James Desarden was caught in a scandal involving the King's eldest son, probably politically motivated and the work of the New Urban Mesh. It cemented London's association with spycraft and became the 1968 film *Mr Desarden, Prince Consort*. Nackon Day today has a Ministry for the Future. [82.5, 90.1, 101.5, 215.5]

**NEW WORLD COMPANY.** Founded at Morrack in 1463 by Malik Munir al-Hamdawi as a rival to Ambrose III's New Navarre Enterprise. Its ship *Orion* sailed to Aden in 1468 and met Chinese merchants at Mombasa. After 1471 it turned west, found the faster southern route to the Novomund, reached the Arcabil first, opened trade with the young Mashick Empire, and first wrote down the name of Taisha. [56.5, 122.5, 171.5, 218.5, 244.5]
