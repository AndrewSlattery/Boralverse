239.5: Events of 1824

notes on various things that occurred over the course of 1824 (recalling that the Nuntial calendar year begins on March 1).

The Majoric canon Antal Berzéthy announced on 1824 April 3 that he had deciphered the Mysarnos Codex. Berzéthy had spent eleven years with the copy of the first folio held at the Library of Sobjan [Pécs], and his method treated the Codex's glyphs as a numerical cipher of Church Latin, each shape standing for a value and each value for a letter by a rule of his own devising. The translation he published ran to sixty pages of devotional commonplaces on patience, industry and the flight of the soul. It was received warmly. The Sobjan chapter struck a medal, and for most of that summer the Codex was considered a solved problem and a disappointing one.

A magistrate in Boxa [Novi Sad] heard argument on 1824 May 19 in the prosecution of the printer Sava Bogdanich for the publication of indecent matter. The Riverine Cities had been a federacy for barely a year and their courts were newer still, and the case turned almost entirely on a single word. The prosecution held that tov, appearing eleven times in the offending pamphlet, was a term of the tovaric [queer] demimonde and therefore an indecency on its face. Bogdanich's advocate produced Danil Manjurich's Vernacular of Boxa of the previous year, which defines tov as "a loving salutation between brothers…avoided in polite company", and argued that a word a lexicographer could not bring himself to define could not be relied upon by a court to convict a man. The magistrate agreed and dismissed the charge. The judgment is the earliest surviving use of the word in a public document anywhere, and the Promethean clubs of the city treated it as a licence, which it was not.

The opera Ale in Fiamme (Wings Afire) was first performed at the Verona Arena on 1824 June 12. Its librettist and composer Chiarina Bruno, born on her father's estate in western Furlaine and resident in Verona for most of her adult life, had taken the story of Icarus and given its final act to a device nobody in the audience had heard before. As Icarus climbs, a consort of viols placed outside the Arena and tuned deliberately flat plays a slow figure which the boy alone can hear, and which he takes for the encouragement of the gods. These are the graveyard fiddlers (Borlish viol cemtir), the death-spirits of Borlish folk belief whose music is heard near cemeteries in the hour before dawn. Bruno had the tale from her twin brother Jacomo, who had served alongside Borlish troops at the Battle of Hulm three years earlier and who was in the audience that night. The two were noted for a resemblance remarkable even in twins, and Jacomo is said to have been congratulated on the opera some dozen times over the course of the evening without troubling to correct anybody.

The physician Berenguer Solsona reported to the Barcelon port authority on 1824 August 22 that the sickness aboard two vessels lately arrived from the Gurjati ports was pennackit [cholera], which had by then been moving west along the trade roads for seven years. The quarantine that followed was thorough, late and useless; cases were ashore before the order was signed. Better remembered than the outbreak is the argument about it. Preachers of the Modest Arrangement across Vascony and the Spanish peninsula held the sickness to be a judgment on the loosening of morals, and were widely believed. The Promethean clubs replied that the disease had crossed six kingdoms of the pious without pausing to inspect their conduct. Neither party had the least idea what caused it, and the exchange is nonetheless usually counted among the opening moves of the Deviance movement's rise in the south.

Word reached the Steeplepost Tapestry at Aiga d'Angoixa [~Quebec City] on 1824 October 5 that Governor Guilhem Arrosteguy had died at his residence. The message came up the southern flag-lamp chain in proper form and under a proper station mark, and by early afternoon the Tapestry was showing a mourning device across its full face — several myriad tiles turned to black but for a white cross, an image that a great many people in the square below remembered for the rest of their lives. Bells were rung in the city and in six towns along the Oxagre. The governor, who was in good health and at that moment some forty leagues upriver at Illa de Vinna Salvatge, learned of his own death two days later.

The messages did not stop. Over the following week the southern chain carried a report of unrest at the New Provincial border, then of a column moving north, and at last on 1824 October 11 an alleged declaration of war from New Provence, which the Tapestry displayed in text at first light. Militia were called out in three districts. The whole of it collapsed the next morning when a packet out of Gorram tied up at Aiga d'Angoixa carrying mail, newspapers and a New Provincial trade delegation, none of which had heard of any war. The subsequent inquiry established that a relay clerk at Sent Aunès had been paid to insert messages into the chain at a point where no station could see far enough in either direction to contradict him, and that the broker Amadeu Lascorz had bought Ambrosian government paper heavily through the week and sold it on the fourteenth. Lascorz was never convicted of anything. The clerk was, and the Ambrosian steeplemesh acquired within the year a system of station keys, counter-signatures and dead intervals which made the chain slower, duller and considerably harder to lie on. The Tapestry itself never fully recovered its authority, and spent the rest of its working life on short government notices and the occasional diced sketch.

The farm theorist Ricardo Ivarrachezurí read a paper to the agricultural society at Huzatre [Bayonne] on 1824 November 20 concerning the failure of a breeding programme he had run for four seasons. Ivarrachezurí, who had fought as a mozardisto during the Second German War and turned to livestock afterwards, had been storing collected seed in vessels of several metals to determine which best preserved it, and reported that "the seed of the bull, the ram and the stallion alike are made impotent in storing them for some hours in a syringe of copper". He connected the effect correctly to the same property by which copper vessels resist spoiling, and treated the whole business as an obstacle to be engineered around. The society agreed with him. That the passage describes a workable contraceptive was not remarked upon in print by anybody for another forty years, and when it finally was, it was by people who had never heard of him.

Darren Brodus, then twenty-six and lately of the New Vithor University, published a short letter on 1824 December 14 which ended the Berzéthy decipherment. Brodus did not dispute a single one of the canon's readings. He instead applied Berzéthy's own rule, allowing himself the same freedom of choice the canon had allowed himself, to four pages of a Vithor shipping register, and obtained from it a passage of devotional commonplaces on patience, industry and the flight of the soul. The letter is four hundred words long and is still set as an exercise. Berzéthy never replied to it and never withdrew his translation; the Sobjan medal is in the library's collection to this day, catalogued without comment.

The corporation of London opened the second of the new south-bank embankments on 1824 January 30, a work begun during the seventy-fifth anniversary building of 1823 and finished considerably over its estimate. The engineer Hester Wadlow had argued for the deeper river wall against a committee that wanted a cheaper one, and won by the expedient of publishing the committee's own soundings alongside her own. What the embankments did to the price of land on the Suthwerk side is the reason the free city's south bank looks as it does, and is also the reason a great many people who had bought quietly along it in 1822 were rich by 1830.

An assayer at Killdara in the Irish tallath of Britain, Gwilim ap Trahaern, separated on 1824 February 11 a heavy dark earth from the pitchy ore of the local workings and gave it no name beyond kildárc, "the Killdara stuff", which is the name it still has. He established that it was not lead, not silver and not of any use he could determine, and sold the greater part of his sample to a pottery at Baleock, which found that it made a fine yellow-green glaze. Killdark spent the following century colouring dinner plates and window glass. What else it could be made to do was not suspected by anybody until the discovery of the atomic nux, and was not demonstrated until the lifetimes of people now living.

[CAPPATIA — slots in as an opener, before Berzéthy]

The Portingale factory at Marambá on the northern coast of Cappatia loaded eleven hundred waterproofed capes, four hundred pairs of boots and some two thousand bottles of cured coscou [rubber] aboard the Esperança do Sul on 1824 March 22. The goods were excellent and the factory had not made them; they were bought from the river peoples, who cure the latex in smoke over clay forms and had been doing so for longer than anybody at Marambá thought to ask. The ship crossed the line in April. By Sant Adrian [Bioko] the cargo had become one object, and the Lisbon buyers, who had paid in advance for capes, declined to take delivery of a hold. Duarte Sanguinhal, who had put four years and his wife's money into the venture, did not recover. His was neither the first such loss nor the last; through the following fifteen years the same cargo was shipped by six or seven houses in much the same way, melting in the tropics and going to powder in a northern winter, until somebody at last worked out how to stop the stuff from remembering that it had once been a liquid.

[CATHAY — slots in around July]

The printer Sen Pauquo of Tanjeu completed on 1824 July 9 a run of two thousand copies of the Ninefold Magpie, a collection of tapsue tales assembled from chapbooks, recitation and his own memory. Tapsue ("magpie") stories are the old Cathayan entertainment of the elaborate theft, in which the thief is admired, the victim is rich and faintly deserving, and the object taken is very often returned by a route nobody could have predicted. Sen printed his in maniocan, the syllabary that had spread through Cathay under the Kitans as a deliberate severance from the south, and which can be learned in two years rather than ten; the edition was therefore legible to servants, and was despised accordingly, and sold out twice before the year turned. Pirated settings were circulating in Leudong within eighteen months. That the tapsue tradition and the masquira romance of Europe resemble each other closely has been remarked upon since the 1890s, and nobody has ever established that either of them knew the other existed.

[ANGELICA — slots in around September]

The council of New Leudong resolved on 1824 September 3 to open the country beyond the Barrangan to settlement, and spent rather more of its deliberation on the form of the grants than on the country. The polity had been settled twice over — from Britain by emigration and from Cathay by design — and had two incompatible habits of holding land, the one a freehold in the British manner and the other a registry of the Cathayan kind in which the record, rather than the deed, is the thing that is true. The compromise arrived at is why New Leudong title is registered and indefeasible today, a century before anywhere else adopted the practice. Both systems, however, required that the land be unheld before it could be granted, and the surveyor Gwenllian ah Idris put it in writing that it was not, naming eleven families, their boundaries and the seasons in which they used them. Her memorandum was received, filed and not answered. The ordinance is still the root of every title on that side of the river. The memorandum was printed for the first time in 1971, and every argument since has begun with it.

[UNGOTIA — slots in as a closer, or anywhere late]

A ship's surgeon laid up at Ungotia [Zanzibar] through the rains finished on 1824 December 28 a word-list of the harbour argot, which he had begun out of boredom and continued out of interest. The argot ran on Suahile [Swahili] stems with Arabic and Gurjati bones and took in whatever the crews of four oceans left behind; the surgeon's forty-one pages are the earliest record of it. Among the entries is valanguri, plural of mlanguri "healer", which he glosses as "one who deals in goods that have no owner". The list was never printed. It went home in a sea-chest, was copied at least three times, and its vocabulary surfaces two decades later in the Glamour of Deganny, whence the word passed into British cant, and thence into Borlish as vlangr, and thence into the mob trials of the 1900s and the ordinary language of everybody who read about them. Nobody in Borland who has ever called a man a vlangr has had the least idea that the word began as a compliment paid to a doctor.
