247.5: Ireland

some collected notes on Ireland and its polities.

Ireland (Guile [Irish] Erin, Welsh Iwerðon, Borlish Irðon) is the westernmost of the three large Northwestern Isles, lying west of Albion [Great Britain] across the Irish Sea. Politically it comprises:

    the Arvor, a coastal stretch in the east which primarily speaks Welsh, contains many of the island's larger towns and cities and is considered part of Wales for the purposes of government;
    Connaught and Munster, two Irish-speaking, federated tallaths [provinces] of the Kingdom of Britain which divide the majority of the island into roughly equal northern and southern halves.

History of the first millennium

Veneration of the abbess Brigid of Killdara [Kildare] was early and widespread. The abbey at Rexam [Wrexham] in Wales was dedicated to her; its original building burned in 1301, when Andrew II of Markland reignited the Mallor Wars.

Ireland's monasteries were comparatively insular, especially in comparison to those of Borland. The most important of the Irish annals is the Ultaugh [Ulster] Chronicle, kept at the monastery of Benghor [Bangor, Co. Down]. With the Chronicle of Carlile [Carlisle] it is one of the two principal sources for the Dane centuries around the Irish Sea; both, for example, record the partition of Mondwell [Anglesey] agreed between Wynneth [Gwynedd] and Sodrick [Man and the Hebrides].

Danes settled in Ireland during the Norrish [Norse] ascendancy of the eighth and ninth centuries. The wayports and settlements between the Dane kingdoms of Dublin and Yorwick [York] carried a great deal of traffic.

Under King Roderick, Dunclothe [Strathclyde] took Arran in 934 and Mann in 942. The expansion caused lasting acrimony with some Irish kingdoms, since the kings of Ultaugh had taken tribute from these for generations.

Squeezed between Tyrconnal to the west and the Dane ports on its own coast, Ultaugh was broken apart in the years around 1020. Its last king, Oghy the Hoary, was killed in 1021, and the land was divded between Tyrconnal inland, Lenster in the south and Danes along the eastern coast.

By 1028 there were four Irish kingdoms of any power: Tyrconnal, Connaught, Lenster and Munster. Tyrconnal and Lenster were fighting wars at once with the Norrish to the east and the other Irish to the west, while the houses of Connaught and Munster vied for the position of High King of Ireland. Dublin, meanwhile, belonged with Dunclothe and Sodrick to a loose confederation of Dane kingdoms.
