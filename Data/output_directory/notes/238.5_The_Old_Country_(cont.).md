In 1121 the arrangement nearly collapsed—Vigo VII, king of Devon, launched a failed invasion of the Tremonnow [Monmouth] region without the Emperor's blessing (Roger II was in Paris facing a depleted treasury and a Flemish impasse).

Alan VI split Devon from Kernow in the realignment of 1225, some decades after the disintegration of the Drengot Empire, and Wales spent the rest of the century collecting counties. In 1280 the lord Ivan of Cartaw [Cardiff] conquered what remained of Devon of (it seems) his own accord—his king, David, showed some rare good sense and decided he had authorised the act all along. Thus he declared the Tallath of Devon and granted it to Ivan's son Marduth, Ivan having died of a fever before he could enjoy his spoils.

Salt and sail

Through the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, while the Mallor Wars between Wales and Markland raged once more (not for nothing does the city of Rexam/Car Vantel [Wrexham] have two unrelated names), the fishermen of Dunclothe were trawling the northern banks for cod. Access to the hungry Middlesea [Mediterranean] markets required salt for curing, and lords grew wealthy on the flow of salt north and fish south. They also accumulated coin on a scale unmatched since the Roman days.

Such quantities of coin bought Wales a new harbour at Aberdaglethow [Milford Haven], which had been a fishing village and which became by 1420 the second-largest port in Albion [Great Britain] after London. Even more consequentially, the people (or at least the nobility) of Wales, Dunclothe, Sodrick, Kernow, Devon and the eastern Irish coast acquired the habit of thinking of themselves as having common political interests.

British fishing can also indirectly be credited—or blamed—for the Vetomundane [Old World] landfall on Mendeva [N Am] in 1471, for it was a desire to displace their position in the fishing trade that led Vascony under Ambrose III to create the New Navarre Enterprise and to explore the northern Atlantic further that anyone since the Danes.
