240.5: False Friends (cont. 6)

some more dictionary entries, this time concerning plants and animals, from the Concise Onomatory o'Merch (seventh edition), a reference work published in 2011 and compiled by the Utcheter [Uttoxeter] Lexical Association.

- behemoth [/bɪˈhiː.mʊθ/ "hippopotamus"] n. a large-bodied, broad-nosed and river-swimming ferent [mammal] of Africa; anything of unwieldy size (for example, a trevold [novel]); a large and ungainly person; (religion) a great beast appearing in the book of Job. Early Merch in the scriptural sense < Late Latin behemoth < Hebrew בְּהֵמוֹת • behēmōth, apparently a plural of בְּהֵמָה • behēmā "beast". Applied to the ferent from c. 1640, after scholars identified the beast of Job with that animal, via a (now considered spurious) Egyptian p'eḥe'mau "water ox". In this sense, displaced earlier riverhorse < Greek ἱπποπόταμος • hippopótamos.

- clause [/kləuz/ "gene"] a condition forming a distinct part of a legal document; (life) a length of heredian acid [DNA] carrying the method for some particular trait or function of a living thing; (language) a phrase considered as an indivisible unit of the formal structure of a sentence. Early Merch clause "sentence" < Old French clause < Medieval Latin clausa "close of a rhetorical period" < Latin claudō "I close". In life theory from 1865, originally in the phrase heredian clause, extending the textual metaphor of Rachel Desselut's Parable of the Mute Bookbinder (1743), in which living matter contains a written method for its own construction (compare heredian volume [chromosome]).

dragon [/ˈdɾa.gʊn/ "dinosaur"] n. any of a group of lizards which filled the lands and seas until some sixty-six hundred myriad years ago, known chiefly from fossil remains and their surviving progeny the birds; (historical) a serpent of myth and legend, often winged, fire-breathing and hoarding treasure; (colloquial) a fierce, formidable or intimidating person; v. intr. of the breath, to show as vapour in cold air. 13C, early Merch dragoun < Old French dragon < Latin dracō "serpent, dragon" < Greek δράκων • drákōn "serpent", perhaps < δέρκομαι • dérkomai "I see clearly". Used principally in the real-world sense since the mid-nineteenth century, based on the usage since 1826 in the writings of Portingale rock theorist Maria Lourinnan.

mause [/məuz/ "banana"] n. (pl. mause) the elongated fruit of a particular tropical plant with pale flesh inside a rind which ripens from green to yellow; the plant bearing this fruit, a tall treelike herb with broad leaves (also mauser or mause tree); a starchier variety of this fruit eaten cooked (usually cooking mause). 1530, clipped from earlier mauser fruit (15C) < Middle Borlish mauser "mause-tree" < maus < Arabic مَوْز • mawz < Middle Persian mōz < Sanscrit मोच • mōcha < Tāmir mōttai < ocean trade from further east. The word entered the English languages via Borlish trade through the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries.
