235.5: Duel over the Stars

excerpt from Pleas of the Axbane Shrievalty during the reign of Brandon III, 1548–61, an annotated compilation of trial transcripts prepared by historian of law Anna Bureldin (during her employment with the New Vithor University) and published in 2016.

…the ninth day of February, 1555N. The townsman Marc Levosclin is summoned [1]. The factor Jagm Ossory complains of a debt of eleven solt at the table of inn-mistress Aliç Trewen on the eve of Saint Timothy [2].

Levosclin contends the money was lost at a game of lot, and further that the cards were bastard; Ossory rejoins that the game was of wit [3], and prays the judge will hear it described.

Aliç Trewen (sworn): each has ten cards. Coin [4] is master over the other four ensigns. Whoever takes the most hands takes also all the wagered money. A hand containing the Duel or the Stars is counted twice.
Dirc de Maes, a patron originally of Anvers [Antwerp, 5]: concurs with Trewen, save that the Stars can only be taken by a coin or the Duel, and not merely by a more senior card.
Trewen (recalled): claims she has not heard this rule, but will not swear that it is false. When asked whether the game may be won by one whose play is unskilled, she says she has seen it done, but not often, and not…

===

[1] This case has the distinction (according to Jonnovar Meyaunot's 1982 work Crossing the Rubicon) of containing the earliest description of a card game using the Maritime deck. Meyaunot is also responsible for the (utterly unsubstantiated) perception that Levosclin was a professional gamester. In fact, nothing is known of him outside these records, and the surname is otherwise unattested.

[2] Likely the twenty-sixth day of January, although the indeterminate uptake of the reformed Nuntial calendar in sixteenth-century Borland muddies the dating.

[3] City statute forbade the imposition of debts greater than one solt due to "games of lot" (joy de lot), but not due to "games of wit" (joy d'engin). The meaning of "bastard" (nothe) here appears to be "doctored".

[4] Here referring to the coin (scet) ensign [card suit], not to actual coins.

[5] Possibly the same "Dirk van Masz" referenced as a dye merchant in the correspondence of Anscon d'Aupont.
