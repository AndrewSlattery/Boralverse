246.5: The Pocket Vetomund

entries from The Pocket Vetomund, a single-volume encyclopedia of Afro-Eurasian history and places. It was first published in 1934 by Winchester Scholastic Primers, the press behind The Alphabetical Albion, and the entries below are taken from its fourth edition (1996).

HULM, BATTLE OF (1821)
An offensive of the Second German War (1815–23) fought at Hulm [Ulm]. On one side fought Bavarn and its allies, among them Borland, Crovatia and Furlain [Friuli] (the latter having opposed Bavarn earlier in the war).

The Borlish mathematician Darren Brodus, who served there, wrote in a letter of 1824 that it was "an astonishingly-mismanaged offensive" which "prolonged the war for months". Among his comrades was the Furlain landowner Jacomo Bruno, twin brother of the composer Chiarina Bruno, with whom Brodus kept up a lifelong correspondence.

The city itself is better known for its university, which has held since 1770 the first true self-describing book, duplicated once every four years, and which in 1819 published Desselut's posthumous *The Tellure and the Tellard*.

LITHOW
A land on the eastern coast of the Graven Sea [Baltic]. It was the primary target of the Baptism of the North in the thirteenth century, when Rome, fearing that the Augsburg See would reach the north first, sent forces from Crain [~Slovenia] and Borland to convert it. The Convoy Australier later founded a chapter there.

The eighteenth-century Lithowan Marion Hartman is remembered for helping to end the Russian War for the Graven, and by botanists for visually identifying the case kernel [cell nucleus].

In 1859 Tomas Vilmanas designed the Vilmanas Teleclavia in Lithow, the first tachygraph [typewriter] able to send messages on the steeplemesh; its first message, the opening line of Ovid's *Heroines*, went to Margreta Legaite in Tvaniste, who had to receive it by hand.

RODULF OF KENT (d. 1028)
Kent's king for nearly eight decades; he came to the throne as a child. Ruling from Canterbury, his Kent subdued the eastern half of Wessex and held a recognised supremacy over the remainder, Devon contested it. His long deathbed through 1027 and 1028 left the succession deeply uncertain. His eventual death triggered the Kernovan Conjuring of 1029, in which Normandy's house Drengot conquered Kent with the help of Greater Devon's house Kernow, and thus his death helped bring about the First Drengot Empire.
