244.5: The Two Chinas

collected notes on Cathay and China, sometimes known together as the Chinas.

Cathay (Borlish Cathay) and China (Borlish Scin) are the two great polities of eastern Asia. They have sometimes been known elliptically as the Northern Empire and the Southern Empire, respectively. While the region had formerly been united on several occasions, Cathay and China have been separate since the late fourteenth century.

The name Cathay reflects the name of the Kitan, which travelled overland via Turk languages and Persian Khitāi to Medieval Latin Cataya; the name China is likely a dynastic name carried by sea around the coast of India to Sanscrit Cīna and Persian Chīn. The two names were attached to what were believed to be separate realms long before the final sundering.

Cathay owes its present form to the Leu dynasty founded by the Kitan people in the tenth century. This period also sees the first soundwise use of Sinic runes [Chinese characters], long practised for foreign names and words, gaining currency for general use (but not in formal writing). Cathay conquering the divided south piecemeal through the thirteenth century, though initially successful, is thought to have caused sufficient resentment that (when the Leu soon collapsed) the south quickly consolidated under the Southern Emperors.

News of western exploration reached the Southern Empire in 1468, when Chinese merchants at Mombasa met the crew of the Morrack ship Orion. Nearly fifty years later, Chinese traders guided the British ship Phœbos on to Gurjat [Gujarat], Madray [Madurai] and Borune [Borneo]. In 1520, hearing that Morrack and Britain planned to sail round the globe and loth to be outdone, the Dinghoi Emperor (r. 1511–1534) issued the Hangthen Edict (行天). In 1528 a treasure ship became the first Chinese vessel to alight in the Novomund [New World], on the Lovac [Pacific] coast of Mendeva [N Am]. The voyages of this era are the subject of Quoming's The Sea Peoples, an epic of exploration considered one of the greatest Chinese works ever written.

In the seventeenth century the trade mesh of Gurjat [Gujarat] reached as far east as Cathay. After the Angelican Landfall, Cathayan state-building alongside emigration from Britain formed the polity of New Leudong in south-eastern Angelica [Australia], christened by the Cathayan captain Niv Kizun after the Leudong [Liaodong] peninsula.

Several outbreaks of disease in China in the century before 1735 are now suspected to have been pennackit dysentery [cholera]. In the Democratic Wars of the mid-eighteenth century China was embroiled in the contest for India, and it launched attacks on the eastern Indian polities through one of its protectorates.
