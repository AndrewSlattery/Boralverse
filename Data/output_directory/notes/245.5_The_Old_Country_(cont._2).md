245.5: The Old Country (cont. 2)

continued excerpt (covering the years 1424 to 1517) from The Old Country, a tourist guidebook about Britain [~Wales et al.] aimed at travellers from British Mendeva [~US South] written by Ossewma'h Ariandro.

…founding a kingdom when they formed a unified fleet. The lords of the tallaths [provinces] had spent a generation learning that they needed an ultimate authority (and scapegoat) for the navy and its contracts.

The tallaths kept their own courts, levies, taxes and languages, and gave the crown only their navies, their coinage and their ambassadors. The final oaths were sworn on David's Day, rendering the New Year, the patron's day and the founding of the kingdom a single festival. (This goes some way to explaining Deganny [Conwy].)

The Wars of Fealty

The Wars of Fealty began over the Redstone Chair, a throne which Britain had neither claim to nor use for. Nonetheless, Britain joined from a position of strength and spent the early years extracting concessions from Markland. At Rexam [Wrexham] in 1483 the last Duke of Chester was killed, and when the attack on Chester itself was rebuffed shortly afterwards, Britain—with the border more or less where it wanted it—bowed out, leaving the rest of Albion [Great Britain] to fight on for another thirty years.

The marriages of this period are illustratively prestigious. Margaret of Iscombe [Exeter] married Prince Mark of Tolose, heir to Vascony, so that Ambrose the Ascendant and his elder sister Alexandra had a Devoner mother. Alexandra's second marriage, made during the wars but before her famous voyage to the Novomund, was into Wales.

Howel's road

King Howel spent the subsequent peace on harbours. By the turn of the century a chain of British victualling stations ran down the Atlantic coast of Africa, built to reach Asia directly by sea.

The Phœbos, captained by Martan mac Kellot, went down that road and kept going, via Ungotia [Zanzibar] and Gurjat [Gujarat], where it spent several months of 1512, and on through Madray [Madurai]. In 1516 it reached Borune [Borneo], and at Sinquan [Singkawang] the following year the Morrack [Moroccan] ship Cynthia found it at anchor.

The popular story that the two crews could not understand each other is wrong; a dozen men aboard the Phœbos had some Arabic. What actually passed between the ships is unknown, because the relevant pages of al-Casmi's otherwise immaculate logbook are missing.
