243.5: Borlish Articles and Demonstratives

Borlish nouns don't inflect at all for number, gender or case. Number and definiteness are carried by articles and demonstratives.

The Definite Article

The definite article is ‹y› before a consonant (including a pronounced ‹h›). Before a vowel, a silent ‹h› or consonantal ‹y›, it is elided to ‹l'›, which is written joined to the following word.

    ‹y mur | y hayennaç | l'esc | l'hostel | l'yenç›
    /i mɪr | i ˌhe.jɛˈnats | lɛx | lɔsˈtɛl | ljɛnts/
    'the wall | the workload | the bait | the hotel | the arrival'

Three common prepositions fuse with y before a consonant:

    ‹ag | dy | ny›
    /ɛj | di | ni/
    'to the | of the | in the'

Before a vowel, a larger (but still closed) set of prepositions fuse with l'.

    ‹all' dell' nell' | coll' sull' poll' pall' | entrell' contrell' | soll'›
    'to the, of the, in the | with the, on the, for the, by the | between the, against the | under the'

Fusion only happens when the noun phrase is the preposition's complement. When a preposition introduces an infinitive clause, the infinitive's object comes first and so may directly follow the preposition. Then the article stays separate: y primer a y mont circonnavigar 'the first to circumnavigate the world'.

A noun phrase with y may be singular or plural. If its number shows at all, it shows in verb agreement.

    ‹Y tissour aun un exeunt scenað.›
    /i tɪˈsur on ɪn ɛkˈsawnt xeˈnaθ/
    the weaver have.3PL a walkout stage-PTCP
    'The weavers have staged a walkout.'

The Indefinite Article

The indefinite article is un, identical to the numeral 'one'. It contracts with the same three prepositions as y: an 'to a', d'un 'of a', n'un 'in a'.

    ‹Dou rout ranscellaurn n'un bosc gauvn.›
    /du rut ˌran.xɛˈlorn nɪn bɔx ˈgo.vn̩/
    two road fork-PST-3p in.a wood yellow
    'Two roads diverged in a yellow wood.'

Indefinite plurals are either bare or take a demonstrative (see below) or a quantifier like pieç 'some'.

The Demonstratives

Borlish has four demonstratives, distinguishing near from far and singular from plural.

    ‹oc ec | oç eç›
    /ɔk ɛk | ɔts ɛts/
    'this these | that those'

Before a noun, a demonstrative normally follows the definite article, which (since the demonstratives all begin with a vowel) is always l' or one of its fused forms: l'oc, dell'oc, all'oç, pall'eç. Since the noun itself never changes, this combination is the main way to mark a definite noun phrase as singular or plural.

    ‹L'oc scuir pasc. | L'ec scuir pascn.›
    /lɔk xajr pax | lɛk xajr ˈpax.n̩/
    'This boy eats. | These boys eat.'

In this role the "pointing" force of the demonstrative is often weak, and English is better served by a plain 'the'.

    ‹L'ec voç de nossy felau echovan tras y pereð.›
    /lɛk vɔts de nɔˈsi feˈlo ˌe.koˈvan traz i peˈrɛθ/
    the=these voice of our mate resound-IPFV.3PL across the bulkhead
    'The voices of our mates carried through the partition.'

Without the article, the plural demonstratives mark an indefinite plural, like an English bare plural.

    ‹Y regnanç er ec hom mixallant a casc jorn.›
    /i rijˈnants ɛr ɛk ɔm ˌmɪ.ksaˈlant a kax ʒɔrn/
    the regime be.IPFV these person guillotine-PTCP at each day
    'The regime was beheading people every day.'

Bare demonstratives also turn up in expressions of time: oc aubon 'this morning', oc annað 'this year', ec noit 'at night', oç noit 'overnight'.

Finally, the demonstratives can stand alone as pronouns. Normally they take the article, so l'oc 'this (one)' and l'oç 'that (one)' behave like any other noun phrase. In a more literary style, the bare forms are placed before the verb as objects, just like object pronouns.

    ‹Ða gent oc saun, y sar marcer dourra mal.›
    /ða ʒɛnt ɔk son i sar marˈtsɛr duˈra mal/
    if people this know-3PL the net commercial hurt-FUT badly
    'If people find out about this, the trade network will suffer.'
