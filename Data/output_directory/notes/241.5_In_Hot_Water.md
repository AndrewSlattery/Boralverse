241.5: In Hot Water

further excerpt from Borland before Borlish (Istr Boral pre Borallesc), a history textbook published in 1983 through the New Vithor University, taken from its chapter on the lead curse tablets recovered from the Roman spring at Axbane.

…surviving writing from Roman Borlish was written either by elites or by soldiers. The curse tables cast into the Axbane spring are by some way the largest single exception. These were written or dictated by ordinary men and women of the town, and principally ask for the restitution of stolen property or the punishment of thieves. The hoard was discovered underneath the Jaibos brewery in 1878, in the course of deepening a well.

Petitioners dedicated stolen property to the spring's goddess or to Nahalennia, goddess of travellers and the ocean, so that the theft became hers to avenge. They would ask that thieves lose sleep and health until the goods were returned. Bath-attendants were often prime suspects—I give you Tertius. He says he saw nothing. Let him then see nothing until my cloak is found. (Tab. 98). Other tablets are written in mirror-writing to ask that a rival's health and fortunes be reversed; one asks to make a mule-thief "cold and heavy, like this very tablet" (the usual material for curse tablets being lead).

We can also chart a religious shift over time. Early tablets name local deities like Nahalennia, also seen as Nalennia (Tab. 27), who was worshipped also across the strait in Gallia Belgica, especially by salt merchants. Later tablets are more commonly to pan-Roman deities like Proserpina, with whom it is possible that Nahalennia was syncretised—Tab. 176 appears to call Nahalennia the consort of Dis Pater (that is, Pluto, god of the underworld).

There is one tablet mentioning Christianity—…struck down whether he be Christian or no… (Tab. 67). The silt over the tablets was sealed by a layer of broken masonry that included pieces of two altars, which appear to have been deliberately thrown in.
