7. Continents

Since the early nineteenth century geographers have divided the large landmasses of the world into three realms: the Vetomund, the Novomund and the Ceteromund. We treat them in the order that names for them entered the Merch language.

Novomund [/ˈnɔ.vʊ.mʊnd/] n. the continents of Cappatia [S Am] and Mendeva [N Am], as well as many islands including the Arcabil [Caribbean]. 1645 < Scholastic Latin novomundus < 1509 phrase dē novō mundō "from the new world".

The name of the northern Novomundane continent, Mendeva, is attested (in the obsoleted adjectival form mendēvalēnsis) on maps from the 1471 Landfall voyage made by Vascon fishing trawlers expanding their reach in the north Atlantic. It derives from Vask [Basque] mendeval "west", itself from Vascon phrase vent de val "valley wind".

By contrast, the name of Cappatia is seen only from the 1570s. It enters Merch via Latin cappatiānus and Arabic from Run'allo [Quechua] cappacha "this world (as opposed to an underworld or a celestial world)", via contact between Morrack merchants and the Tavancine [Inca] Empire in the sixteenth century.

Vetomund [/ˈnɔ.vʊ.mʊnd/] n. the continents of Africa, Asia and Europe, as well as their off-lying islands. 1689 on world maps (1692 in print) < Scholastic Latin vetomundus "old world", formed in the manner of novomundus, by analogy.

The three Vetomundane continents appear in Merch throughout its existence and back into Old English. They descend directly from Latin.
Africa has its roots in Latin Āfer, usually taken to be from a Punic name for their people related to 𐤏𐤐𐤓 • afar "dust".
Asia comes from Greek Ἀσία • Asíā "Anatole [Anatolia]", likely from Hittite 𒀸𒋗𒉿 • Assua "northwest Anatole".
Europe comes from a princess of Greek myth, whose name may either reflect Greek εὐρεῖα ὤψ • eureia ōps "broad face" or a relative of Aramaic ערובא • arūva "sunset".

Ceteromund [/ˈsiː.tə.ɾʊ.mʊnd/] n. the continents of Angelica [Australia] and Australia [Antarctica], as well as landmasses such as Tecamaw [Aotearoa NZ]. 1772 in theoretical use for any as-yet uncategorised lands < Scholastic Latin cēteromundus "the other world", from cēterus "rest, remainder".

Angelica's name, first attested in Latin in the eighteenth century, is an alteration of earlier Angalica, Angalaca, originally applied only to the northern coast of the continent. In this sense it comes via Arabic and Bāsan [Indonesian] from a local language, perhaps Dual Jungle [~Yolŋu Matha] āngangaraca "ancestral lands, lit. bone country".
