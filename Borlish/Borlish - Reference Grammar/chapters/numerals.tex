\chapter{Numerals}\label{ch:numerals}

This chapter describes the cardinal and ordinal numerals, the collective and fractional numerals, numerals in the noun phrase, and the expression of years and clock times.

The numbers from eleven to nineteen end in \bl{-oç} or \bl{-ç}, and the tens from forty upwards in \bl{-ant}. \bl{Un} `one' is also the indefinite article (Section~\ref{sec:indef-article}), and \bl{dou} `two' with the article is `both' (\bl{y dou}). \bl{Zefr} `zero' can also mean `no, nought'.

\subsection{Compound numerals}
Compound numerals are formed by simple juxtaposition, largest element first, with no conjunction: \bl{trent un} `thirty-one', \bl{cent cinq} `a hundred and five', \bl{carant dou} `forty-two'.
    \glsen{Y stuðant teyen veint un eð y caðer teyen cent cinq.}{i stiˈðant tiˈjɛn vint ɪn ɛθ i kaˈðɛr tiˈjɛn tsɛnt tsɪnk}{{\Def} student {number-\Ipfv.\Third\Pl} twenty one and {\Def} chair {number-\Ipfv.\Third\Pl} hundred five}{There were twenty-one students and a hundred and five chairs.}

\subsection{Large numbers}
Numbers above a hundred are counted in hundreds, up to ninety-nine hundred: \bl{veint tre cent carant dou} `2342' (literally `twenty-three hundred forty-two'). Counting in thousands, as in \bl{dou mil tre cent carant dou}, is somewhat old-fashioned. The exception is a number whose hundreds place is zero, which is counted in thousands: \bl{dou mil} `two thousand', not \bl{*veint cent}.

The next unit is \bl{murðon} `myriad, ten thousand' (Merch \textit{mirthon}): \bl{dessur de murðon pavar} `over ten thousand pavar'. Higher powers of ten are multiples of it: \bl{deg murðon} `a hundred thousand', \bl{cent murðon} `a million' and \bl{mil murðon} `ten million'. So, together with counting in hundreds, large numbers are grouped in fours rather than threes. A number above a myriad names the myriads first, then the rest by the usual rules, including the reading in thousands when the hundreds place is zero: \bl{un murðon veint tre cent carant cinq} `12,345', but \bl{un murðon dou mil carant cinq} `12,045'. A larger count of myriads comes first in the same way: \bl{doç murðon trent catr cent cinquant seg} `123,456'. In figures, the digits are grouped in fours to match, with a comma after the myriads: \bl{12,3456 hom} `123,456 people'. A count of myriads above a hundred is read in the same way: \bl{125,0000} is \bl{cent veint cinq murðon} `1,250,000'.
    \glsen{Sceyen cent veint cinq murðon hom ny citað.}{xiˈjɛn tsɛnt vint tsɪnk mɪrˈðɔn ɔm ni tsiˈtaθ}{{there.be-\Ipfv.\Third\Pl} hundred twenty five myriad person {in.\Def} city}{There were 1,250,000 people in the city.}

\section{Numerals in the noun phrase}\label{sec:numerals-np}
A cardinal numeral stands before the noun, which has no plural marking: \bl{dou fraðr} `two brothers', \bl{deg annað} `ten years'. A numeral may follow a determiner, as in \bl{y dou} `the two, both' (\bl{dont y dou son sanat} `both of whom are doctors').

The usual way of stating how many there are of something is with the verb \bl{teir} `number, come to', whose subject is the group counted. The existential \bl{sceir} `there be' can be used as well, but is less idiomatic.
    \glsen{L'eç hom ig teyen veint atorn.}{lɛts ɔm aj tiˈjɛn vint aˈtɔrn}{{\Def=\Dist.\Pl} person there {number-\Ipfv.\Third\Pl} twenty about}{There were about twenty people there.}
`About' with a number is \bl{atorn}, placed after the numeral (\bl{veint atorn}, \bl{all'hour ogt atorn} `at about eight'), or \bl{atorn de} before a numeral and noun (\bl{Sceyen atorn de veint hom ig}). `More than' is \bl{may a}, with the comparative standard marker \bl{a} (Chapter~\ref{ch:comparison}).
    \glsen{May a veint hom venoirn.}{me a vint ɔm veˈnɔjrn̩}{more than twenty person {come-\Pst.\Third\Pl}}{More than twenty people came.}
Colloquial speech avoids a modified numeral directly before its noun, and prefers either a collective numeral with \bl{de} (\bl{May an veintaç d'hom venoirn} `More than a score of people came') or the numeral phrase after the noun (\bl{Eç hom may a veint venoirn}).

\section{Ordinal numerals}\label{sec:ordinals}
Each of the first ten ordinals has two forms: a short form inherited from Old Borlish (\bl{tarç} continues Latin \bl{tertius}), and a later, mostly regular long form built on the cardinal with \bl{-em} (\bl{treyem}, from \bl{tre}). The two are in complementary distribution: the short form goes before the noun, the long form after it.

    \glsen{L'oc es my tarç sojorn a Rom.}{lɔk ɛz mi tarts soˈʒɔrn a rɔm}{{\Def=\Prox.\Sg} be {\First\Sg.\Poss} third trip to Rome}{This is my third visit to Rome.}
The same is \bl{my sojorn treyem a Rom}, with the long form after the noun, or \bl{my sojorn a Rom treyem}, treating \bl{sojorn a Rom} `trip to Rome' as a unit. The short forms are the more formal, but they are still widely used in speech, especially up to `fourth'.

Several short forms have other uses. \bl{Cart} is also `quarter' (Section~\ref{sec:time}), \bl{degm} `tithe, tenth part', and \bl{scon} appears in \bl{scon stemn} `second (cousin)', literally `second lineage'.

A predicative ordinal takes the article: \bl{Nossy tourm surcou y dovem} `Our team came second'. As an adverb, an ordinal takes \bl{cos}, the adverbial marker: \bl{Jo pau y lox cos dovem} `I ate the pizza second'.
    \glsen{Nossy tourm surcou y dovem.}{nɔˈsi turm sɪrˈku i ˈdo.vɛm}{{\First\Pl.\Poss} team {turn.out-\Pst} {\Def} second}{Our team came second.}

\section{Collective numerals and fractions}\label{sec:collectives}
Collective numerals are nouns meaning `a group of about N'. They are formed with \bl{-aç} on the tens (\bl{veintaç} `a score, twenty-odd'). On other numbers the suffix sounds odd. The counted noun follows with \bl{de}: \bl{an veintaç d'hom} `a score of people'. \bl{Doçan} `dozen' also means `many', and bare \bl{doçan de} is `dozens of': \bl{Sceyen doçan d'hom, ma for un manne de caðer} `There were dozens of people, but only a handful of chairs'. \bl{Un des y mil} is `one in a million'.

Some tens and teens also serve as nouns for a period of that many years or a person's age, as \bl{carant} `forty; period of forty years; one's forties' and \bl{quinç} `fifteen; fifteen-year-old'. \bl{Ogtoç} is also `a group of eighteen'.

`Half' is \bl{demeg}, a noun: \bl{tre e demeg} `three and a half', \bl{un hour e demeg} `an hour and a half', \bl{un demeg d'hour} `half an hour', \bl{demeg d'un lau} `half a league'. \bl{Mitað} `half, side' is also used. `Quarter' is \bl{cart}, the short ordinal.

\section{Years and dates}\label{sec:years}
`In' a year is \bl{d'an} `of the year' with the numeral. Years are read in hundreds like any other number (Section~\ref{sec:cardinals}): \bl{d'an novoç cent ogtant catr} `in 1984', \bl{novoç cent trent un} `1931'. The reading in thousands, \bl{mil nouf cent ogtant catr}, is old-fashioned.
    \glsen{D'an novoç cent ogtant catr y denzan dy toun teyen veint tre cent carant dou.}{dan noˈvɔts tsɛnt ɔjˈtant katr̩ i dɛnˈzan di tun tiˈjɛn vint tre tsɛnt kaˈrant du}{{of=year} nineteen hundred eighty four {\Def} inhabitant {of.\Def} village {number-\Ipfv.\Third\Pl} twenty three hundred forty two}{In 1984 the village had 2,342 inhabitants.}
A century is named with the cardinal after \bl{siecr}: \bl{ny siecr quinç} `in the fifteenth century', \bl{y preu demeg dy siecr novoç} `the first half of the nineteenth century'. A millennium, by contrast, takes the short ordinal before the noun: \bl{ny preu demeg dy scon millenner} `in the first half of the second millennium'.
    \glsen{Rom ny siecr quinç sta ven d'orðenjanç.}{rɔm ni ˈsjɛ.kr̩ kwɪnts sta vɛn ˌdɔr.ðɛnˈʒants}{Rome {in.\Def} century fifteen {be.\Ipfv} vein {of=invention}}{Fifteenth-century Rome was a hotbed of innovation.}
A day of the week takes \bl{je} `on the day of' for a single occasion, past or future (\bl{Je Sol vam nos ag play} `On Sunday we're going to the seaside'), and \bl{ec}, the plural demonstrative, for a habit (\bl{ec Sol} `on Sundays'). `Yesterday' and `tomorrow' are \bl{yer} and \bl{souscen}, or more familiarly \bl{je ados} and \bl{je posc}, `the day behind' and `the day after'.

\section{Clock times}\label{sec:time}
The time is given with \bl{cair} and \bl{l'hour} `the hour' as its subject, in the verb--subject order of weather expressions (Section~\ref{sec:impersonal}). `What time is it?' is \bl{Cay l'hour alcot?}, literally `The hour falls how much?'.
    \glsen{Cay l'hour tre e demeg.}{ke lur tre e deˈmij}{fall {\Def=hour} three and half}{It's half past three.}
Minutes past the hour are added with \bl{e} `and' (\bl{set e deg} `ten past seven'), and minutes to the hour are subtracted with \bl{lon} `without' (\bl{seg lon cart} `a quarter to six', literally `six lacking a quarter'). `At' a time is \bl{a} with the number, or \bl{all'hour} `at the hour'. `Noon' is \bl{haçol}, and `in the evening' \bl{ag crevoscr}.
    \glsen{Y convey part a seg lon cart eð yem a set e deg.}{i kɔnˈvi part a sij lɔn kart ɛθ jɛm a sɛt e dij}{{\Def} train leave at six without quarter and arrive at seven and ten}{The train leaves at a quarter to six and arrives at ten past seven.}
