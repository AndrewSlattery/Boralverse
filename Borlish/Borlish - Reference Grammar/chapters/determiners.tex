\chapter{Articles and Demonstratives}\label{ch:determiners}

Borlish nouns are inflected neither for number nor for definiteness (Chapter~\ref{ch:nouns}). Both are instead expressed by the determiners that precede the noun: the definite article \bl{y}, the indefinite article \bl{un}, and the demonstratives \bl{oc ec oç eç}, which double as the language's main means of marking number. This chapter also covers the quantifiers that occupy the same position before the noun. Possessive determiners are treated in Chapter~\ref{ch:pronouns} and numerals in Chapter~\ref{ch:numerals}, but their interaction with the article is described here.

In glosses, a preposition or possessive fused with the article is split into its parts: \bl{ag} is glossed `to.\Def', and \bl{mell'} `\First\Sg.\Poss-\Def='.

\section{The definite article}\label{sec:def-article}

\subsection{Form}
The definite article is \bl{y} \phm{i} before a consonant, including a pronounced \orth{h}. Before a vowel, a silent \orth{h} (Section~\ref{sec:orth-consonants}) or the semivowel \orth{y}, it is elided to \bl{l'} \phm{l}, written joined to the following word.
    \glphm{y mur | y hayennaç | l'hom | l'hostel | l'yenç}{i mɪr | i ˌhe.jɛˈnats | lɔm | lɔsˈtɛl | ljɛnts}{{\Def} wall | {\Def} workload | {\Def=person} | {\Def=hotel} | {\Def=arrival}}
Because \bl{y} is pronounced as a vowel, a word with a separate form before vowels takes that form before the article. In particular, `and' is \bl{eð} rather than \bl{e} before \bl{y} (Chapter~\ref{ch:particles}).

\subsection{Contraction with prepositions}\label{sec:art-contraction}
Three common prepositions fuse with \bl{y} before a consonant: \bl{a} `to, at' gives \bl{ag} \phm{ɛj}, \bl{de} `of, from' gives \bl{dy} \phm{di}, and \bl{ne} `in' gives \bl{ny} \phm{ni}. Other prepositions stand apart from the article, as in \bl{por y} `for the', \bl{sur y} `on the' and \bl{con y} `with the'.

Before a vowel, a larger but still closed set of prepositions fuse with \bl{l'}, giving the forms in \bl{-ll'} listed in the table below. The shape of the fused form is regular. A preposition ending in a vowel adds \bl{-ll'} (\bl{de}, \bl{dell'}); one ending in \bl{-n} or \bl{-r} replaces that consonant (\bl{con}, \bl{coll'}; \bl{sur}, \bl{sull'}); and one ending in syllabic \bl{-r} adds \bl{-ell'} (\bl{entr}, \bl{entrell'}). Note the irregular \bl{sou}, \bl{soll'}. Which prepositions fuse is not predictable from their form, however. All others keep the article separate, including the vowel-final \bl{parmy} `among': \bl{des l'} `out of the, since the', \bl{vars l'} `towards the', \bl{posc l'}, \bl{tras l'}, \bl{cas l'}, \bl{durant l'}, \bl{lonc l'}, \bl{jusc l'}, \bl{com l'}, \bl{jos l'}, \bl{ant l'}, \bl{avant l'}, \bl{doutr l'}, \bl{parmy l'}.
\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{l l l l}
        \multicolumn{4}{c}{Preposition and definite article} \\
        \hline
        & & before a consonant & before a vowel \\
        \hline
        & & \bl{y} & \bl{l'} \\
        \bl{a} & `to, at' & \bl{ag} & \bl{all'} \\
        \bl{de} & `of, from' & \bl{dy} & \bl{dell'} \\
        \bl{ne} & `in' & \bl{ny} & \bl{nell'} \\
        \bl{con} & `with' & \bl{con y} & \bl{coll'} \\
        \bl{sur} & `on' & \bl{sur y} & \bl{sull'} \\
        \bl{par} & `by, through' & \bl{par y} & \bl{pall'} \\
        \bl{por} & `for' & \bl{por y} & \bl{poll'} \\
        \bl{pre} & `before' & \bl{pre y} & \bl{prell'} \\
        \bl{sou} & `under' & \bl{sou y} & \bl{soll'} \\
        \bl{entr} & `between' & \bl{entr y} & \bl{entrell'} \\
        \bl{contr} & `against' & \bl{contr y} & \bl{contrell'} \\
        \bl{deur} & `away from' & \bl{deur y} & \bl{deull'} \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}
    \glsen{Casc ivan nell'amaçgat reçoirn un bricot dy colluy indrec.}{kax iˈvan neˌla.matsˈgat reˈtsɔj.rn̩ ɪn briˈkɔt di koˈlaj ɪnˈdrɛk}{each child {in.\Def=nursery} {get-\Pst.\Third\Pl} {\Indf} gift {of.\Def} guild charitable}{Every child in the nursery received a gift from the charity.}
    \glsen{Cal porra balanç intermanir ag tramou ny porçonment?}{kal poˈra baˈlants ɪnˌtɛr.maˈnɪr ɛj traˈmu ni ˌpɔr.tsɔnˈmɛnt}{what {can-\Fut} recompense {constitute-\Inf} {to.\Def} shortfall {in.\Def} allocation}{How will we make up for the budget shortfall?}
    \glsen{Toð y cort ern connoscent de sy ligaçon coll'aðm dy rey.}{tɔθ i kɔrt ɛrn̩ ˌkɔ.noˈxɛnt de si ˌli.gaˈtsɔn kɔˈlaðm̩ di ri}{all {\Def} court {\Cop.\Ipfv-\Third\Pl} {know-\Prs.\Ptcp} of {\Third\Sg.\Poss} liaison {with.\Def=mistress} {of.\Def} king}{The whole court knew of his affair with the king's mistress.}
The contraction takes place only when the noun phrase is the complement of the preposition itself. When a preposition such as \bl{a}, \bl{de} or \bl{por} introduces an infinitive clause, the object of the infinitive comes first (Chapter~\ref{ch:simple-clause}) and so may directly follow the preposition. The article then stays separate, both before a consonant (\bl{a y}, not \bl{ag}) and before a vowel (\bl{a l'}, \bl{por l'}, not \bl{all'}, \bl{poll'}).
    \glsen{L'aye un pedisecq offiçað a y roum costojar.}{lɛˈje ɪn ˌpe.diˈzɛk ˌɔ.fiˈtsaθ a i rum ˌkɔ.stoˈʒar}{{\Third\Sg.\M=have-\Ipfv} {\Indf} goon {set-\Pst.\Ptcp} to {\Def} room {protect-\Inf}}{He had set a lackey to guard the room.}
    \glsen{Tu vel nos aïðar a l'ec fil cardonar ou?}{ti vɛl nɔz ˌɛj.iˈðar a lɛk fɪl ˌkar.doˈnar u}{{\Second\Sg} {will.\Sbjv} {\First\Pl} {help-\Inf} to {\Def=\Prox.\Pl} wire {card-\Inf} {\Q}}{Can you help us untangle these wires?}

\subsection{The article with possessives and quantifiers}
The possessive determiners \bl{my, ty, sy, nossy, vossy} and \bl{lorry} exclude the article before a consonant: \bl{my fraðr} `my brother', \bl{sy ligaçon} `his liaison'. Before a vowel, however, they fuse with \bl{l'} just as prepositions do, giving \bl{mell', tell', sell', nossell', vossell'} and \bl{lorrell'}.
    \glsen{Jo kigla reuvr a mell'orin pre oc annað.}{ʒo kajˈla ˈraw.vr̩ a ˌmɛ.loˈrɪn pre ɔk aˈnaθ}{{\First\Sg} {fail-\Ipfv} {go.home-\Inf} to {\First\Sg.\Poss-\Def=homeland} before {\Prox.\Sg} year}{I hadn't been back home until this year.}
The quantifier \bl{toð} `all, the whole' is likewise followed by the article, and fuses with it before a vowel as \bl{toll'} (Section~\ref{sec:quantifiers}).
    \glsen{Nos javigaum toð y majon livr por detaxion cas aur.}{nɔz ˌʒa.viˈgom tɔθ i maˈʒɔn ˈli.vr̩ pɔr ˌde.takˈsjɔn kaz or}{{\First\Pl} {trawl-\Pst-\First\Pl} all {\Def} house book for synthesis regarding gold}{We searched the whole library for syntheses involving gold.}

\subsection{Uses of the definite article}
The definite article marks a noun phrase whose referent is identifiable. Because nouns do not inflect for number, a noun phrase with \bl{y} may be singular or plural; its number is shown, if at all, by agreement on the verb.
    \glsen{Y tissour aun un exeunt scenað.}{i tɪˈsur on ɪn ɛkˈsawnt xeˈnaθ}{{\Def} weaver {have.\Third\Pl} {\Indf} walkout {stage-\Pst.\Ptcp}}{The weavers have staged a walkout.}
    \glsen{Yeðrot zajadau scanoirn y mur ne bric.}{jeðˈrɔt ˌza.ʒaˈdo xaˈnɔj.rn̩ i mɪr ne brɪk}{ivy variegated {climb-\Pst.\Third\Pl} {\Def} wall in brick}{Variegated ivy grew up the brick walls.}
Generic statements normally use the bare noun. The article with the numeral \bl{dou} `two' gives \bl{y dou} `both', which may precede the noun like any determiner or follow the phrase it refers to.
    \glsen{Tramou aun tagl eð asmuth y dou.}{traˈmu on tɛjl ɛθ azˈmɪθ i du}{vector {have.\Third\Pl} length and direction {\Def} two}{A vector has both size and direction.}
    \glsen{Y dou tarnaç laundaurn ne sortir.}{i du tarˈnats lonˈdorn̩ ne sɔrˈtɪr}{{\Def} two regular {slip-\Pst-\Third\Pl} in {go.out-\Inf}}{The two regulars stumbled as they left.}
Personal names and the names of most countries, cities and islands take no article: \bl{Istr Boral}, \bl{Damvath}, \bl{de Vascony} `of Vascony', \bl{n'Ambrosia} `in Ambrosia'. Several other kinds of name do take it:
\begin{itemize}
    \item rivers and seas: \bl{y Nil} `the Nile', \bl{y Sein} `the Seine', \bl{l'Orn} `the Orne'; \bl{l'Atlantic}, \bl{l'Adriaç} `the Adriatic', \bl{l'Austmar} `the Baltic';
    \item heavenly bodies, compass regions and large regions: \bl{y Tar} `the Earth', \bl{y Lun} `the Moon', \bl{y Sol} `the Sun'; \bl{y Norð} `the North', \bl{l'Aust} `the East'; \bl{y Novomond} `the Novomund', \bl{l'Arcabil};
    \item historical periods, events and wars: \bl{y Paç Lonc} `the Long Peace', \bl{y Rix Romannesc} `the Romantic Wars', \bl{l'Anarchy Portingal} `the Portingale Anarchy';
    \item institutions, organisations, states and documents with descriptive names: \bl{l'Empir Constant} `the Constant Empire', \bl{y Concert Norðer} `the Northern Concert', \bl{y Decret Sandrin} `the Sandrine Decree';
    \item buildings, works of art and other named objects: \bl{dy Majon Roug} `of the Red House', \bl{y Caðer} `the (Redstone) Chair';
    \item ships: \bl{l'Entrpris} `the Enterprise';
    \item titles, and epithets after a name: \bl{y Duc} `the Duke', \bl{Savino y Just} `Savino the Just'.
\end{itemize}
    \glsen{Nos erau y Nil gaddant aval n'auror.}{nɔz eˈro i nɪl gaˈdant aˈval noˈrɔr}{{\First\Pl} {\Cop.\Ipfv-\First\Pl} {\Def} Nile {screw-\Prs.\Ptcp} downstream {in=sunrise}}{We were motoring down the Nile at sunrise.}
    \glsen{La passau y quoncein par y Novomond aðendrar.}{la paˈso i kwɔnˈtsin par i ˌno.voˈmɔnd ˌa.ðɛnˈdrar}{{\Third\Sg.\F} {spend-\Pst} {\Def} twenties by {\Def} Novomund {tour-\Inf}}{She spent her twenties travelling the Americas.}
    \glsen{Saveð vos cal praxitel gravau y Macchine di Helio?}{saˈvɛθ vo kal ˌprak.siˈtɛl graˈvo i ˌma.kiˈne di heˈljo}{{know-\Second\Pl} {\Second\Pl} which sculptor {carve-\Pst} {\Def} Macchine di Helio}{Do you know which sculptor carved the Machines of Helios?}

\section{The indefinite article}\label{sec:indef-article}
The indefinite article is \bl{un} \phm{ɪn}, identical to the numeral `one'. It has a single form, used before vowels and consonants alike (as in \bl{un exeunt} `a walkout' above).

It contracts with the same three prepositions as the definite article: \bl{a} and \bl{un} give \bl{an} \phm{an}, \bl{de} and \bl{un} give \bl{d'un} \phm{dɪn}, and \bl{ne} and \bl{un} give \bl{n'un} \phm{nɪn}. Other prepositions do not contract: \bl{con un}, \bl{par un}, \bl{por un}, \bl{pre un}.
    \glsen{Jo vel hur dar an udomintel.}{ʒo vɛl hɪr dar an iˌdo.mɪnˈtɛl}{{\First\Sg} {will.\Sbjv} hire {give.\Inf} {to.\Indf} babysitter}{I would like to hire a babysitter.}
    \glsen{J'ay me caut orbeutað d'un coroncr ovart.}{ʒe me kot ˌor.bawˈtaθ dɪn koˈrɔnk oˈvart}{{\First\Sg=have.\First\Sg} {\First\Sg.\Obl} {get.\Pst.\Ptcp} {con-\Pst.\Ptcp} {of.\Indf} pretender open}{I've been swindled by a blatant impostor.}
    \glsen{Dou rout ranscellau n'un bosc gauvn.}{du rut ˌran.xɛˈlo nɪn bɔx ˈgo.vn̩}{two road {fork-\Pst} {in.\Indf} wood yellow}{Two roads diverged in a yellow wood.}
There is no plural indefinite article. Indefinite plurals and mass nouns appear either bare, as \bl{yeðrot} `ivy' above, or with the plural demonstrative \bl{ec} (Section~\ref{sec:dem-without-art}) or a quantifier such as \bl{pieç} `some' (Section~\ref{sec:quantifiers}).
A specific indefinite, `a certain, a particular', is expressed with \bl{un certan}, as in \bl{un certan volum} `a particular volume'. A noun that is non-specific, or stands for a kind rather than an individual, takes \bl{untal} `such a, some such' (Section~\ref{sec:quantifiers}).

\section{The demonstratives}\label{sec:demonstratives}
Borlish has four demonstratives, distinguishing proximal from distal and singular from plural.
\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{l c c}
        \multicolumn{3}{c}{Demonstratives} \\
        \hline
        & singular & plural \\
        \hline
        proximal & \bl{oc} \phm{ɔk} & \bl{ec} \phm{ɛk} \\
        distal & \bl{oç} \phm{ɔts} & \bl{eç} \phm{ɛts} \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}
The proximal forms continue Latin \bl{hoc} and \bl{haec}, the neuter singular and plural of \bl{hic} `this'. The distal forms arose in late Old Borlish from \bl{oc i} and \bl{ec i}, with the adverb \bl{i} `there', since lost (Appendix~\ref{app:historical-phonology}).

\subsection{Demonstratives with the article}
Used before a noun, a demonstrative normally follows the definite article. Since the demonstratives begin with a vowel, the article always has the form \bl{l'}, or one of the fused forms of the previous section: \bl{l'oc}, \bl{dell'oc}, \bl{all'oç}, \bl{pall'eç}, \bl{nossell'ec}.
    \glsen{L'oc darfour quesq es tan quemant.}{lɔk darˈfur kwɛsk ɛz tan kweˈmant}{{\Def=\Prox.\Sg} towel damson be so comfortable}{This purple bathrobe is so comfy.}
    \glsen{Vail l'oç quagl star y quagl pascent?}{vel lɔts kwɛjl star i kwɛjl ˈpa.xn̩t}{{be.worth.\Sbjv} {\Def=\Dist.\Sg} quail {be.\Inf} {\Def} curds {eat-\Prs.\Ptcp}}{Should that quail be eating the curds?}
    \glsen{Impar my fraðr j'am l'oç pascman a manoscart.}{ɪmˈpar mi ˈfra.ðr̩ ʒam lɔts ˌpaxˈman a ˌma.noˈxart}{unlike {\First\Sg.\Poss} brother {\First\Sg=like} {\Def=\Dist.\Sg} restaurant at hybrid}{Unlike my brother I like that fusion restaurant.}
    \glsen{Sceye all'oç asc pog avoccaçon a phasm.}{xiˈje aˈlɔts ax pɔj ˌa.vɔ.kaˈtsɔn a ˈfazm̩}{{there.be-\Ipfv} {at.\Def=\Dist.\Sg} age few entertainment at TV}{There wasn't much TV entertainment then.}

\subsection{Demonstratives and number}\label{sec:dem-number}
Since number is not marked on the noun, the article combined with a demonstrative is the principal way of marking a definite noun phrase as singular or plural.
    \glsen{L'oc scuir pasc.}{lɔk xajr pax}{{\Def=\Prox.\Sg} boy eat}{This boy eats.}
    \glsen{L'ec scuir pascn.}{lɛk xajr ˈpax.n̩}{{\Def=\Prox.\Pl} boy {eat-\Third\Pl}}{These boys eat.}
In this role the deictic force of the demonstrative is often weak, and English may be better served by a plain definite.
    \glsen{L'ec voç de nossy felau echovan tras y pereð.}{lɛk vɔts de nɔˈsi feˈlo ˌe.koˈvan traz i peˈrɛθ}{{\Def=\Prox.\Pl} voice of {\First\Pl.\Poss} mate {resound-\Ipfv.\Third\Pl} across {\Def} bulkhead}{Our mates' voices carried through the partition.}
Where the deictic force is present, the plural distinguishes \bl{l'ec} `these' from \bl{l'eç} `those', just as the singular distinguishes \bl{l'oc} `this' from \bl{l'oç} `that'.
    \glsen{L'ec dougl nos mestein cascun.}{lɛk ˈdujl nɔz mɛˈstin kaˈxɪn}{{\Def=\Prox.\Pl} bereavement {\First\Pl} {sadden-\Third\Pl} {each.one}}{These losses sadden us all.}
    \glsen{Jo vos lau ag treu dell'eç xiphar.}{ʒo vɔz lo ɛj traw dɛˈlɛts ksiˈfar}{{\First\Sg} {\Second\Pl} praise {to.\Def} loyalty {of.\Def=\Dist.\Pl} henchman}{I commend you on the loyalty of those henchmen.}

\subsection{Demonstratives without the article}\label{sec:dem-without-art}
Without the article, the plural demonstratives mark an indefinite plural, corresponding to an English bare plural.
    \glsen{Y belem ny forest se mis afaç con ec ciscr d'aucel.}{i beˈlɛm ni foˈrɛst se mɪz aˈfats kɔn ɛk tsɪxr̩ doˈtsɛl}{{\Def} scene {in.\Def} forest {\Refl} {put.\Pst} forward with {\Prox.\Pl} squeal {of=bird}}{The forest scene opened with shrieks from birds.}
    \glsen{Y regnanç er ec hom mixallant a casc jorn.}{i rijˈnants ɛr ɛk ɔm ˌmɪ.ksaˈlant a kax ʒɔrn}{{\Def} regime {\Cop.\Ipfv} {\Prox.\Pl} person {guillotine-\Prs.\Ptcp} at each day}{The regime was beheading people every day.}
The bare demonstratives also appear in expressions of time, such as \bl{oc aubon} `this morning' and \bl{oc annað} `this year' (in the example above).
    \glsen{Y ðocaç lour coy de dormir ec noit.}{i ðoˈkats lur kɔj de dɔrˈmɪr ɛk nɔjt}{{\Def} trauma {\Third\Pl.\Obl} stop from {sleep-\Inf} {\Prox.\Pl} night}{The trauma keeps them up at night.}
    \glsen{Attenað y zachet vel quaglar oç noit.}{ˌa.teˈnaθ i zaˈkɛt vɛl kwɛjˈlar ɔts nɔjt}{{wait-\Second\Pl.\Sbjv} {\Def} glue {will.\Sbjv} {set-\Inf} {\Dist.\Sg} night}{Wait for the glue to set overnight.}
Finally, the demonstratives can stand alone as pronouns. In a somewhat literary construction, the bare forms \bl{oc, ec, oç} and \bl{eç} are used as objects and placed before the verb, like the object clitics (Chapter~\ref{ch:pronouns}).
    \glsen{Ða gent oc saun, y sar marcer dourra mal.}{ða ʒɛnt ɔk son i sar marˈtsɛr duˈra mal}{if people {\Prox.\Sg} {know-\Third\Pl} {\Def} net commercial {hurt-\Fut} badly}{If people find out about this, the trade network will be affected badly.}
    \glsen{L'arra oç saut ig caye sy mariðaç dovem nienter.}{laˈra ɔts sot aj kɛˈje si ˌma.riˈðats ˈdo.vɛm njɛnˈtɛr}{{\Third\Sg.\M=have.\Fut} {\Dist.\Sg} {know.\Pst.\Ptcp} {\Comp} {fall-\Ipfv} {\Third\Sg.\Poss} marriage second void}{He must have known his second marriage was void.}
After a copula, by contrast, bare \bl{oc} follows the verb and stands in for a predicate, much like English \textit{so}: \bl{Paul Lacan es hom prest a savir ig bempart dy mont no's oc} `Paul Lacan knows that most things aren't (fair)' (Section~\ref{sec:perfect-star}).

In ordinary usage the pronoun takes the article, and \bl{l'oc} `this (one)' and \bl{l'oç} `that (one)' behave like any other noun phrase.
    \glsen{Jo so l'oc beint por my dolour youdar.}{ʒo so lɔk bint pɔr mi doˈlur juˈdar}{{\First\Sg} {be.\First\Sg} {\Def=\Prox.\Sg} {drink.\Prs.\Ptcp} for {\First\Sg.\Poss} pain {numb-\Inf}}{I'm drinking this to numb the pain.}
    \glsen{Lodrignether l'oç no's meðes un pescot.}{loˌdraj.neˈθɛr lɔts nɔz meˈðɛz ɪn peˈxɔt}{technically {\Def=\Dist.\Sg} {\Neg=be} even {\Indf} fish}{Technically that's not even a fish.}
The pronoun \bl{l'oç} also heads free relative clauses, `what, that which' (Chapter~\ref{ch:relative-clauses}).
    \glsen{Jo kiglau l'oç seyon rovað dec j'er bucol hamtant.}{ʒo kajˈlo lɔts siˈjɔn roˈvaθ dɛk ʒɛr biˈkɔl hamˈtant}{{\First\Sg} {miss-\Pst} {\Def=\Dist.\Sg} {\Third.\Agt} {ask-\Pst.\Ptcp} because {\First\Sg=\Cop.\Ipfv} idyll {dwell-\Prs.\Ptcp}}{I missed what he asked because I was daydreaming.}
The form \bl{l'ig} \phm{laj} is not a demonstrative, though it looks like one. It is the form the complementiser \bl{ig} `that' takes after a word ending in a vowel sound (Chapter~\ref{ch:complex-sentences}).

\section{Quantifiers}\label{sec:quantifiers}
Most quantifiers stand in place of the article rather than alongside it. \bl{Casc} `each, every' takes a bare noun; the corresponding pronoun is \bl{cascun} `each one' (as in the example above).
    \glsen{Jamiscað plu, casc hom!}{ˌʒa.miˈxaθ pli kax ɔm}{{complain-\Second\Pl.\Sbjv} {no.more} each person}{Stop complaining, everyone!}
\bl{Toð} `all, the whole' is the exception: it is followed by the article (Section~\ref{sec:def-article}), or by a possessive, as in \bl{toð mell'amig} `all my friends'. Without an article, \bl{toð gent} means `everyone'.
    \glsen{Toð gent saun ig cay ty morrig un sbayour jaçol.}{tɔθ ʒɛnt son aj ke ti mɔˈraj ɪn zbɛˈjur ʒaˈtsɔl}{all people {know-\Third\Pl} {\Comp} fall {\Second\Sg.\Poss} aunt {\Indf} gossip longtime}{Everyone knows your aunt's an inveterate gossip.}
\bl{Pieç} `some, a few' takes a bare noun, but with the article it is partitive, `some of the'.
    \glsen{Me stove pieç parol bisan dar a my fraðr.}{me stoˈve pjɛts paˈrɔl biˈzan dar a mi fraðr̩}{{\First\Sg.\Obl} {must-\Ipfv} some word crisp {give.\Inf} to {\First\Sg.\Poss} brother}{I had to tell my brother some hard truths.}
    \glsen{Y nombler volau pieç y bors sdroyað par blisnot.}{i nɔmˈblɛr voˈlo pjɛts i bɔrz zdrɔˈjaθ par bliˈznɔt}{{\Def} banker {steal-\Pst} some {\Def} fund {store-\Pst.\Ptcp} by eunuch}{The banker stole some of the funds kept in escrow.}
\bl{Alcun} `no, not any' takes a bare noun; \bl{alcun hom} is `nobody'.
    \glsen{Y molin produg alcun zucar des insorgent lo quariçtovaurn.}{i moˈlɪn proˈdaj alˈkɪn ziˈkar dɛz ˌin.sɔrˈgɛnt lo kwaˌrɪts.toˈvorn̩}{{\Def} mill make no sugar since rebel {\Third\Sg.\N.\Acc} {sabotage-\Pst.\Third\Pl}}{The mill hasn't produced any sugar since rebels sabotaged it.}
\bl{Pog} `little, few' and \bl{tan} `so much, so many' also take a bare noun (as in \bl{pog avoccaçon} `not much entertainment' above). Both double as degree words: \bl{un pog} `a little, a bit', \bl{tan} `so' (Chapter~\ref{ch:comparison}).
    \glsen{Nos coum alant a haun posc tan semman.}{nɔz kum aˈlant a hon pɔx tan sɛˈman}{{\First\Pl} {get.\Pst-\First\Pl} landfall at haven after {so.many} week}{We made landfall after so many weeks.}
The interrogative determiner \bl{cal} `which, what' likewise takes a bare noun, as in \bl{cal praxitel} `which sculptor' above.

\bl{Untal} `such (a), some such, a kind of' takes a bare noun, as in \bl{untal jorn commovent} `such an active day' and \bl{ben untal pescot blanc} `or some such whitefish'. It is also used on its own as a pronoun, as in \bl{untal vel jo mariðar} `such a man I would marry'.
    \glsen{Ty fraðr es untal daugnam incaint sceir sy lucr volar.}{ti ˈfra.ðr̩ ɛz ɪnˈtal dogˈnam ɪnˈkent xir si ˈli.kr̩ voˈlar}{{\Second\Sg.\Poss} brother be such dupe prone {there.be-\Inf} {\Third\Sg.\Poss} wealth {steal-\Inf}}{Your brother's the kind of dupe who'll get his money stolen.}
\bl{Certan} `some, certain' takes a bare noun, as in \bl{certan historicour} `some historians', and is partitive `some of' when followed by \bl{de} or \bl{entr}: \bl{certan dell'ec method} `some of these methods', \bl{certan entr lou} `some of them'. With the indefinite article it forms the specific indefinite \bl{un certan} (Section~\ref{sec:indef-article}).
    \glsen{Certan dy niveu catr son y scaunç de melodram parsiscent.}{tsɛrˈtan di niˈvaw ˈkat.r̩ sɔn i xonts de ˌme.loˈdram ˌpar.ziˈxɛnt}{some {of.\Def} set four {be.\Third\Pl} {\Def} writing of musical {study-\Prs.\Ptcp}}{Some in the fourth set are learning musical scriptwriting.}

The remaining quantifiers, \bl{bempart} `many, lots of', \bl{necun} `neither, none', \bl{calscon} `any' and \bl{cuscon} `any, whoever', likewise take a bare noun.

The one exception is \bl{gran part} `most', which is followed by \bl{a} and a full noun phrase, definite or indefinite. The \bl{a} fuses with the article in the usual way (\bl{ag}, \bl{all'}, \bl{an}): \bl{gran part ag dynasty imperial} `most of the imperial dynasty', \bl{gran part all'Emperour Drengoçan} `most of the Drengot emperors'.

\section{Order of determiners}\label{sec:det-order}
The determiners combine before the noun in a fixed order: first \bl{toð} or a possessive, then the article, then a demonstrative, then a numeral. A possessive and the article appear together only before a vowel, where they fuse (\bl{mell'}, \bl{vossell'}). Adjectives normally follow the noun (Chapter~\ref{ch:adjectives}).

    \centering

        \hline
        \bl{toð y cort} & `the whole court' \\
        \bl{toð mell'amig} & `all my friends' \\
        \bl{vossell'ec suggestion} & `your suggestions' \\
        \bl{toll'eç xiphar} & `all those henchmen' \\
        \bl{y dou tarnaç} & `the two regulars' \\

\end{table}
