The Augsburg See (Borlish **Sce a Augsbourg**) is the papal seat at [[Augsburg]] that broke from the [[Roman Papacy]] in the [[German Secession]] of the thirteenth century. It has its own line of popes, beginning with [[Paul I/XI]], and did not recognise the Roman Papacy's legitimacy. The lands under its religious authority are known as the Augsburg dominions.

## History
### The Secession
In 1225 Paul von Burchardt, a personal friend of Augsburg's mayor Wilhelm Brüz, wrote the [[Pauline Denunciations]] and declared a *sedes sanctissima* in Augsburg, becoming its first pope as Paul I/XI. Brüz's ambitions for his city are thought to have shaped some of the religious policy in the Denunciations. The years between that declaration and Rome's capitulation in 1251 are frustratingly ill-documented, for obvious reasons, and much of the sequence has to be reconstructed from outside commentary, such as that of the Brethin Houses to the west and the exiled Lombards among the Hellenes. The Secession led to the permanent formalisation of the See as independent of Rome, and it is often considered one of the most influential religious developments of the second millennium.

Thirteenth-century [[Bavarn]] was the epicentre of the schism. In the decades after 1225, each duchy of the [[First German Empire]], and in some cases each county, declared either for Rome or for Augsburg, and the resulting patchwork of allegiances brought the empire down in spectacular fashion. Fearing that Augsburg might spread its heresy into the north faster than Rome could reach it, Rome treated the [[Baptism of the North]] as an urgent undertaking. Religious strife over the schism went on to wrack the polities of Germany and the surrounding regions for centuries.

### Numbering
Whether the Augsburg popes' numbering should include the Roman popes before them, or some other count including any of the antipope lines, was controversial for much of the thirteenth century, so both appellations of the first pope are seen. Paul himself usually included the Roman popes, but the reformist Damien I established that they should not be counted.

## Doctrine and practice
- **Calendar:** One of the Pauline Denunciations objected to Pope Nuntius's reform of the Julian calendar, which had moved the New Year from the Annunciation on 25 March to David's Day on 1 March. Paul called it "the reckless diminution of our ordained measure". The Augsburg dominions accordingly kept the Julian calendar until the much later Rhine Compromises.
- **Zoia:** The late-seventeenth-century theory, popularised by Dufresne and Fayeda, that each generation grew from miniature forms (*zoia*) present since the Creation was, because of the predestination it implied, considered anathema or even heretical in the Augsburg dominions.
- **Cathedral:** In the fourteenth century a cathedral was being built at Augsburg, as was another at Valencia. It has been suggested that the contemporary expansion of Saint Sophia's in Stamboul was commissioned so that it would remain the largest cathedral in Christendom, since either challenger would otherwise have surpassed it.
- **Waduz:** Because of the region's janual religious history, the archbishop of Waduz, a minor city of Bavarn, is said to be the only Christian official who can vote as a cardinal in the conclaves of both the Augsburg and the Roman popes. Conclaves have never yet been called for both at once.

## In culture
The events of the Secession have often been dramatised, most famously and perhaps most inaccurately in the 1961 film *With the North Star to Guide Us*, in which Hunter Noteworthy played Brüz. A magnified statue of Brüz stands outside Augsburg's town hall. It was erected in 1975 and is not based on any extant description or depiction of the man, since none exists.
