Borlish (Borlish **Borallesc**) is the language of [[Borland]] and the only surviving branch of Insular Romance. It shares many features with Gallo-Romance, including the loss of case and intervocalic lenition, and was heavily influenced in the first millennium by Old English and Old Norrish.

## Origins
Borlish descends from the Insular variety of Vulgar Latin, which comprised Borlish Latin and British Latin; it is the only surviving descendant of either. The sound changes from Latin are set out in [[Vulgar Latin to Modern Borlish]].

The language took a handful of words from the Kelt tongue spoken on the island at the time of the Roman conquest, such as **macquar** "raise, rear" (compare Welsh *magu*) and **vrug** "heather" (Welsh *grug*). Far more came from Old English, through Angle settlement after the Roman withdrawal, and from Old Norrish, through Dane settlement in the Viking Age.

## Periods
**Old Borlish** lasted until about the mid-thirteenth century. The vernacular first appears in monastic writing: records of the monk Tobarus (eighth century) are partly in Borlish, and the eighth-century Halvuy chronicle already attests Old Borlish words. In the tenth century alone, Old English took words such as *danny* "nuisance", *bather* "stringed instrument" and *parren* "newcomer" from Old Borlish.

**Middle Borlish** saw the birth of a Borlish literature beyond treaties and religious texts in the late thirteenth century. The 1292 chronicle *The Life of [[Catrin of Kernow]]* (**Y Við a Catrin de Cornou**) is the first long-form work in Borlish about a layperson. The fifteenth-century poet Manuel Kigman, most of whose work survives, is the first attestation of many words. In the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries Borlish trade carried words for traded goods, such as *nargill* and *mauser* fruit, into the English languages.

**Modern Borlish** spelling was last standardised in the reform of 1870. At the time many words had several spellings in use, reflecting more or fewer of the sound changes of the preceding centuries. The reform was guided by the philologist and lexicographer Beatreu Marsac (1828–1902), a lector at the [[Noccair Lyceum]], whose committee kept idiosyncratic features such as coda ‹g› for /j/ against a phonetically-minded minority, while deprecating etymological spellings such as ‹eig› and ‹sç›. English *kevler*, a subgenre of [[Masquira romance|masquira romance]], is a late-nineteenth-century loan from Borlish **romanç kevlar**.

## Grammar
Nouns mark neither gender, case nor number. Number is shown by demonstratives or by verb agreement:

- **l'oc scuir pasc** "this boy eats"
- **l'ec scuir pascn** "these boys eat"

Adjectives have a base and a comparative form ([[Adjectives]]). Stress usually falls on the final syllable ([[Stress]]).

## Varieties
The grammar articles describe the Damvath Standard, and in particular the mesolectal speech of the capital ([[Allophony]]). Several recent sound changes of the Damvath Standard apply very unevenly across the island.

## Writing
The standard alphabet has twenty-seven letters: the Latin letters without ‹w›, plus ‹ç› and ‹ð›. ‹w› appears in foreign words and some names, and the characters ‹æ œ ï› are used in addition ([[Alphabet]]).
