Lithow (Borlish **Lithou**; adjective *Lithowan*, Borlish *lithovan*) is a land on the coast of the [[Graven Sea]] in northeastern [[Europe]]. It was the first target of the [[Baptism of the North]] in the thirteenth century.

## History
### The Baptism of the North
During the [[German Secession]], Rome, fearing that the [[Augsburg See]] might reach the North first, made the Baptism of the North an urgent undertaking. Forces from Rome-allied [[Crain]] and [[Borland]], with Borlish missionaries and the [[Convoy Australier]], converged on Lithow as their first target before going on to its more distant Graven neighbours. The Convoy, which grew substantially in numbers and wealth during the thirteenth century, eventually founded a chapter there.

Agata Naziunas made her homeland of Lithow the particular focus of *A Baptism of Fire: Christianity and the Graven* (1988), published by Leidéiai Givatés, which chronicles the Christianisation of the states and peoples of the Graven coasts.

### Later history
The eighteenth-century Lithowan Marion Hartman, whose scientific diagrams of plant cases label each nucleus *punctulus* "little dot", is better known for helping to end the Russian War for the Graven.

In 1859 Tomas Vilmanas (Borlish *Toman*) designed the Vilmanas Teleclavia in Lithow, the first tachygraph able to send messages on the steeplemesh. Its first message, the opening line of Ovid's *Heroines*, was sent on Michaelmas of that year to his friend Margreta Legaite in Tvaniste, who received it by hand, since no machine could yet transcribe directly from the steeplemesh.

## Language
The Lithowan word *sudrabs* "silver" is cited alongside Hausan *'asrvae* and Vask *zirar* as possible evidence that the word for silver was an ancient trade-word, perhaps from Acadian *surpum*. Lithowan *stabá* "idol" is allied, along with Majoric *stobroċke* "figurine" and Bulgar *stobór* "woodcarving, wooden doll", with the word *stobor*, which first referred to doll-animating sprites in the folklore of Awry and Kiew.
