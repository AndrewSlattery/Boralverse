Normandy (Borlish **Normandy**) is a region in the north of [[Gaul]] and the continental heartland of the polity of [[Kent]], whose capital [[Roun]] lies within it on the Seine. Formerly the Duchy of Normandy, it was the home of the Drengot family, who founded the [[First Drengot Empire]] in the eleventh century. Its western border has long been the river Vire.

## History
### Dane settlement
Viking raids along the Seine and the Orne began in earnest in the ninth century, as part of the wider [[Dane Supremacy]], and by 850 Roun was under Norman control. Danish settlement spread across the north of Gaul in the ninth and tenth centuries, and even beyond the Vire, which had formed part of the border between the Franks and Dumnonia since the eighth-century Catumagin Accords. When the [[Kingdom of the Franks]] formally ceded the fiefdom in the tenth century to form Normandy, the Vire remained the border.

As the Dane Supremacy waned in the tenth century, many people left [[Sodrick]] and other Danish lands for the relative prosperity of Normandy. Norman ships raided along the coasts of [[Europe]] in this period; in 946 itinerant Norman mercenaries sacked [[Marsella]], nominally on behalf of the Malchassian Romans. Norman migrants also moved south, founding towns such as Thelmass, and this continued for some centuries.

### The Duchy
By the eleventh century Normandy was a duchy ruled from Roun by the Drengot family, a semi-autonomous vassal of the Kingdom of the Franks. It stretched from the Vire in the west to [[Flanders]] in the east.

### The Drengot Empire
In the [[Kernovan Conjuring]] of 1029, Duke Roger Drengot conquered Kent with the support of [[Devon|Greater Devon]]'s House of [[Kernow]], following the death of King [[Rodulf of Kent]]. Roger went on to conquer Essex and [[Markland]] and to annex much of the rest of Gaul, and he was crowned Emperor Roger I in 1038.

Roun was sidelined as the emperors moved their court to [[Paris]] during the eleventh century. They continued, however, to own the duchies of Normandy and Kent personally, and this began the lasting political union of the two regions on either side of the Kentish Channel. When a popular uprising in Flanders against Emperor Geoffrey II, believed to have had the backing of local nobility, broke out around 1067, the emperor's supporters in Normandy mustered enough force for the rebellion to be quickly disavowed.

### Norman Kent
When the Drengot Empire was dissolved in 1191, one of its successor states was a united Norman Kent, ruled from Roun. In the mid-fifteenth century King [[Vincent II of Kent|Vincent II]] expanded the city keep there, and his twin sons, the Two Henries, ruled after him. During this period Roun French developed into one of the standard written forms of French. [[France]] spent nearly two decades in growing animosity towards Kent under the Two Henries before the two reached reconciliation in the early 1490s.

In the [[Second Great Dying]] of the early sixteenth century, Norman Kent recorded only about half as many deaths per capita as English Kent and [[Markland]] across the Channel. During the [[Democratic Wars]] of the eighteenth century, the philosopher [[Rachel Desselut]] went into exile in Norman Kent, which at that time meant Normandy and Flanders.

In 1852 Norman Kent united politically with France and [[Burgundy]], and this alliance grew into the [[Drengot Collusion]]. Roun was the Collusion's administrative centre until its collapse in 1961, and housed the [[Red House]], home of the Ministry for Information.

## Language
Norman is one of the two languages of Kent, along with the English language Kentish. Like Paris French it is one of the northern French languages, the *lengas d'oil*. The Roun standard developed in the fifteenth century as one of the written forms of French, alongside Parisian and Burgund French. Many Norman words entered the English languages during the First Drengot Empire, including words based on the Norman names for numerals, and Kentish has had more French influence than any other English language. Borlish has borrowed from Norman words such as *naumagl* "ox" (from *un naumail*) and *caglou* "lens, focus" (from *caillow* "small stone, lens").

## People

- [[Vréqueu Margouille]], who played lineball for Norman Kent in the 1935 Lineball Global Tourney
