Cathay (Borlish **Cathay**), sometimes called the Northern Empire, is one of the two great polities of eastern [[Asia]], north of [[China]]. The two have formerly been united on several occasions, but they have been separate since the late fourteenth century.

## Name
The name Cathay reflects the name of the Kitan people. It travelled overland via the Turk languages and Persian *Khitāi* to Medieval Latin *Cataya*. The names Cathay and China were attached to what were believed to be separate realms long before the final sundering.

## History
Cathay owes its present form to the Leu dynasty, founded by the Kitan in the tenth century. In this period the soundwise use of Sinic runes, long practised for foreign names and words, gained currency for general use, though not in formal writing. One historical-linguistics thesis argues that this was because Cathay under Kitan rule deliberately set itself apart from its southern neighbours. Through the thirteenth century Cathay conquered the divided south piecemeal. The conquest was at first successful, but it is thought to have caused enough resentment that when the Leu soon collapsed, the south quickly consolidated under the Southern Emperors.

In the seventeenth century the trade mesh of [[Gurjat]] reached as far east as Cathay. After the [[Angelican Landfall]], Cathayan state-building, alongside emigration from [[Britain]], formed the polity of [[New Leudong]] in southeastern [[Angelica]]. It was christened by the Cathayan captain Niv Kizun after the Leudong peninsula. New Leudong has seen significant Cathayan nation-building since the late seventeenth century. The writer [[Pether Hetsong]] was of Cathayan and British heritage.

## Culture
- The Cathayan *tapsue* "magpie" tales are an old tradition that some scholars see as a precursor of the [[Masquira romance|masquira]] and kevler stories of nineteenth-century Europe.
- The Cathayan "surveyor's span" is one of the two lengths whose midpoint fixed the dime foot; the other is the Roman foot.
- Cathay, like China and the Persias, has had institutions monitoring the effects of government policy for centuries, arguably millennia.
