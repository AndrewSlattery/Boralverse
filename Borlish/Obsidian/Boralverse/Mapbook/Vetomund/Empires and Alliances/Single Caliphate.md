The Single Caliphate (Borlish **Caliphað Sengr**) was an empire ruled from Cairo, which at its height stretched from [[Spain]] to Persia. It was one of the four empires of the [[Second Tetrarchy]] of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, alongside the [[First Drengot Empire]], the [[First German Empire|German Empire]] and the [[Second Roman Empire]]. It held the African coast of the [[Middlesea]] for much of this period.

## History
### Formation
The Single Caliphate was the product of several centralising reforms in the early eleventh century, by which Cairo consolidated its authority over the various successor states of the Abbasid Caliphate. Its more distant territories, such as the Admiralty of Cordova in Spain and the Turkish lands, were never as closely ruled as its heartland.

### Admiralties
The Caliphate's provinces were admiralties.
- **Ifricia**, the later [[Libya]], was an important admiralty ruled from Tunis. Its port of Trapoles was a major Middlesea port of the Caliphate.
- **Cordova**, in the south of Spain, was ruled by Abdul Raman IV, crowned in 1188. A mapbook of the peninsula after 1198 shows the Admiralty of Cordova with its court at Cordova and other centres at Seville, Murcia and Malaga.

### Learning
The philosophy of the Greeks was studied in the Caliphate, and one history of medicine traces its route into Europe "from Naples to Rome by way of Madine". Nadim of Trapoles, a twelfth-century mathematician at the Observatory in Trapoles, formulated the Trapolitan identity, which for centuries made long multiplications easier. In the twelfth century mathematical texts from the Caliphate were being translated into Latin in great quantities, and the [[Library of Sobjan]], built under King [[Jambor of Awry]], collected many of them. Scholars of the Autonome University of [[Saint Marinus]] were among the first to translate mathematical texts from Arabic into Latin. The Library of Sobjan also holds a copy of a 1261 *liber numerorum* translated from Arabic works written in the Caliphate.

### Decline
In the eleventh and twelfth centuries [[Andalus]] lost significant territory in the north and east to Christian polities such as [[Barcelon]]. In the last decade of the twelfth century Musab as-Sakr of Ispella led an early faction for independence from the Caliphate. His death in a fall at the age of forty postponed such ideas for another two generations. The Brethin nun Isida of Toled (fl. 1220) lived during "the twilight of the Single Caliphate's Cairene authority over Andalus".

As imperial hegemony in Europe and northern Africa fell apart, so did the Single Caliphate. In 1237 (634 AH) an independent admiralty was declared in Libya, at the same time as the Morrove admiralty to the west.

## Places
- Cairo, in [[Egypt]], the seat of the Caliphate
- Tunis, from which Ifricia was ruled
- Trapoles, a major port with an observatory
- Cordova, seat of the admiralty in Spain
