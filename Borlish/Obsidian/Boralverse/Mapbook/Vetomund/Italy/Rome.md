Rome (Borlish **Rom**) is a city in [[Italy]] and the seat of the [[Roman Papacy]]. In antiquity it was the centre of the Roman Empire, and in the Middle Ages it ruled the Papal Dominium. After the Great Fracture of the sixteenth century the city came under the political dominion of the Triunvirato. As a polity, Rome borders [[Tuscany]].

## Ancient Rome
Rome developed diplomatic and trade links with the [[Northwestern Isles]] from the time of Julius Caesar's expeditions to Britannia in 55 aN, and its invasion of the islands began in 42 N. It conquered the whole of [[Borland]], mostly in the second half of the first century, and only part of [[Albion]], and it never attempted [[Ireland]]. In the second century Emperor Hadrian pursued different strategies in Britannia and Borland, the latter becoming a separate province under him. In the fourth-century Tetrarchy of Diocletian, Roman Brittania and [[Gaul]] formed the District of Constantius.

The Roman Empire was divided around 450. In 536 Justinian I took the city back from the Eastern Goths, a short-lived reconquest that marks the beginning of the [[Second Roman Empire]]. Both that empire's Malchassian rulers in the east and the Drengot emperors in the west used the Church to support their claims to continuity with Rome.

## The Papal city
By the time of Pope Mercury II in 590, Rome had taken a strategic interest in Borland as a Christian outpost from which to send missionaries to Albion and the Danes. At the beginning of the twelfth century, when Rome's power was unparalleled, the Papal Dominium was ruled from the city, "Seat of all Christendom", by Pope Damian V (1110–1121).

The military catastrophes of the [[Failed Crusade]], declared by Pope Just in 1210, stoked discontent in Rome, as did the First Great Dying, a plague probably brought back by returning crusaders. In 1225 Wilhelm Bruez, mayor of Augsburg, denounced Rome as "the ancient seat of the Christ-killers" (at least in a 1961 film's version of his letter), and that year Paul I/XI declared a rival *sedes sanctissima* in Augsburg. This [[German Secession]] ended with the capitulation of Rome in 1251. Under the more totalist Pope Hermeneus II, Rome came to see the [[Baptism of the North]] as urgent, fearing that Augsburg might spread its heresy there first. The Rome-allied Duchy of [[Crain]] joined Borlish missionaries and the [[Convoy Australier]] in the effort.

## The Great Fracture
Fifteenth-century Rome was a hotbed of innovation, and the city was both a patron and a pioneer of alchemical methods. Yet in the study of Classical mathematics and science, Rome and the other Italian states spent the century before 1550 largely playing catch-up. They lacked the Novomundane wealth that funded new inventors elsewhere, and their unchecked monarchs held them back, Rome most of all.

In the sixteenth century central authorities in many places took advantage of the local nobility's declining power to consolidate their own. It has been argued that this process in Rome led directly to the Great Fracture of the mid-sixteenth century. Afterwards the Pope remained, on paper, an absolute ruler of scattered provinces elsewhere in Italy and Germany, but in practice the city of Rome came under the political dominion of the new Triunvirato.

The Triunvirato swiftly dissolved the city's old prohibitions on blasphemous arts and philosophical publications, and invited great minds from across Europe, probably less from zeal for discovery than to bring new political factions into the city against the See. The first Brethin House in Italy was established in this period.

Under the nose of a divided Church, Rome briefly became a haven for open cosmological speculation. Martèl fi Bacchero, a mathematician at the library of Saint Jerome, was debating a Sun-centred cosmos in the city's public fora by 1552. His closest friend, the sculptor Lorenzo dell'Acchedia, carved *[[I Macchine di Helio]]* in his workshop in Rome, and it was displayed in a nearby public square for its first decades. The city's art of this time belongs to the Revitalist period.

In the mid-seventeenth century the religious politics of the south began to give way to more secular rivalries, and Naples at last found itself free of "the Papal embrace of Rome".
