Italy (Borlish **Italy**) is the peninsula of [[Europe]] that reaches into the [[Middlesea]] east of [[Provence]]. Its polities include the maritime republics of [[Jeans]] and [[Venise]], the kingdom of [[Lombardy]], [[Tuscany]], [[Furlain]] in the north-east, and [[Rome]], seat of the [[Roman Papacy]].

## Medieval Italy
The Second Roman Empire took the city of Rome back from the Eastern Goths under Justinian I, and at its height, during the [[Second Tetrarchy]], it controlled lands as far west as Corse. By then, however, its outlying regions enjoyed significant autonomy, especially where the Papacy's power loomed larger than that of Constantinople. In the north the Kingdom of the North Lombards fell to Lombardy in 987, and its ruling family fled to the Empire. Verona and its province were contested between the Kingdom of the Lombards (later Lombardy) and the [[German Empire]] from the tenth century into the Second Tetrarchy.

Jeans, the oldest of the so-called Latin Republics, came into existence in the tenth century. Together with Venise it is a counterpoint to the notion that the Second Tetrarchy was made up solely of great centralised empires.

A mapbook of the period describes Italy at the beginning of the twelfth century, surrounded by three empires, as "a volatile place". It shows five powers in 1120:
- the Papal Dominium, ruled from Rome by Pope Damian V (1110–1121)
- the Duchy of Tuscany, ruled from Florence by Duchess [[Matilda of Tuscany|Matilda]] (1102–1146) of the Pisan dynasty
- the Kingdom of Lombardy, ruled from Pavia by King Jacob III (1087–1130) of the Verona branch of the Adelchians
- the Republic of Jeans, led by Console Franco Cicala
- the Republic of Venise, at a momentary territorial maximum after taking Ancona and its Corfu holding in 1112

In 1207 Jeans took the Tyrrhene Isles of Corse and Sardine from Roman control, though only Corse remained in its orbit for long. The Autonome University of [[Saint Marinus]] in Lombardy was founded in the eleventh century, and its scholars were among the first to translate mathematical texts from Arabic into Latin. A severe earthquake struck Furlain in 1348, destroying the castle and cathedral of Odin.

## The Revitalist period
The Roman Papacy remained the ultimate authority over Christendom until the [[German Secession]] of the early thirteenth century. The Great Fracture of the mid-sixteenth century severely diminished its temporal power. On paper the Pope remained an absolute ruler of scattered provinces in Italy and Germany, but in practice the city of Rome came under the political dominion of the new Triunvirato.

In the century before 1550, Rome and the other Italian states had mostly been playing catch-up in the study of Classical mathematics and science. They lacked the Novomundane wealth that funded new inventors in Vascony, Kernow and Morrack, and their unchecked monarchs held them back further. The Triunvirato lifted Rome's old prohibitions on blasphemous arts and philosophical publications and invited great minds from across Europe, and the first Brethin House in Italy was established in this period. The result was the Revitalist period, in which artists and scholars drew on the legends of Greece and Rome. Probably its best-known work is the sculpture [[I Macchine di Helio]] by Lorenzo dell'Acchedia.

Piracy plagued the Middlesea coast, and it peaked at the turn of the sixteenth century, as wealth from the [[Novomund]] flooded the region.

## Modern Italy
The Italian Concert was formed in 1891, during the [[Good Game]] period, with the Lombard diplomat Audace Saviano instrumental in its establishment. In the late 1960s and early 1970s, the General Italian Fervour produced a wave of "giftales", fiction about Italian places and people (or the Italian diaspora). Examples include Carla fi Lohis's *A Single Misstep* (1966) and Carlyn van Stede's *While Gold Yet Runs in My Veins* (1965).

## Languages
Italy's languages include the Italian of Tuscany, [[Italian|Venise Italian]], Furlain Italian, Jean and Napolitan. The language of Tuscany has lent many words to other European languages because of the region's prestige and trade wealth. Borlish has taken words such as *broccat* "brocade" and *sbolognar* "to foist upon" from Italian, and Venise Italian *rasgatto* "dénouement" passed into many European languages.
