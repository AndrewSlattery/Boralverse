The Baptism of the North (Borlish **Batesm dy Norð**) was the project to fold the northeasternmost part of [[Europe]] into Christendom, carried out in the thirteenth century among the peoples of the [[Graven Sea]]. The [[Convoy Australier]] led it for several decades, and it became an urgent undertaking for Rome during the [[German Secession]] (1225–1251), when Rome feared that the [[Augsburg See]] might spread its heresy to the North first.

## History
### Beginnings
An Imperial Evangelism of the Far North had already taken place in the twelfth century. After Pope Just revoked the Convoy Australier's rule over Antioch and Port Simon in 1208, the order, formally the Order of Saint Hemma of Still Water, was assigned to the courts of [[Svonimir of Crovatia]] and [[Taxon of Awry]]. This left it ideally placed to lead the Baptism, which it did for several decades with money from Taxon.

### The Secession
In the decades after the Pauline Denunciations of 1225, each duchy of the [[First German Empire]], and in some cases each county, declared either for [[Rome]] or for Augsburg, and the resulting patchwork of allegiances brought the empire down in spectacular fashion. In this context the Baptism became urgent. Under the new and more totalist Pope Hermeneus II (fl. c. 1250), whom one list of popes names as its instigator, Rome feared that Augsburg might spread heresy faster than Rome could reach the North.

The Duchy of [[Crain]], which controlled the prosperous city of Trieste, declared for Rome, probably more from pragmatism than true religious fervour, since it bordered both Lombardy and Crovatia. Whatever its motives, it was perfectly positioned to contribute. Forces from Crain and [[Borland]], with Borlish missionaries and the Convoy, converged on their first target, [[Lithow]], and went on to its more distant Graven neighbours. The Convoy grew substantially in numbers and wealth during the thirteenth century, founding outlying chapters as far afield as Borland and, eventually, Lithow.

### The Inger
The Inger were a pagan holdout. Isida of Aldegia (fl. 1280), a princess of the Inger known locally as Siterø, was an integral part of the Baptism: her marriage to the king of Holmgrod was contingent on her people's conversion. The Inger converted in stages, the elite being baptised some years before the wider population, and Isida is said to have served as interpreter for the Convoy's missionaries in the meantime. Her feast day falls on 21 December, the winter solstice, which most historians hold to reflect a deliberate alignment.

## Historiography
*A Baptism of Fire: Christianity and the Graven* (1988), by Agata Naziunas and published by Leidéiai Givatés, chronicles the changing religious landscape of the Graven peoples from the pre-Christian period through the Imperial Evangelism and the alliances that formed after the Secession, with a particular focus on Naziunas's homeland of Lithow.
