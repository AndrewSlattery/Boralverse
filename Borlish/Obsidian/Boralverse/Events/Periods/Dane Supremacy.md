The Dane Supremacy (Borlish **Paramontað Dannesc**) was the period of Danish raiding, settlement and rule across northern [[Europe]] during the Viking Age, roughly from 800 until the eleventh century. Raiders and settlers from [[Denmark]] and Horth struck the coasts of [[Borland]], [[Albion]], [[Ireland]] and [[Gaul]], and founded lasting kingdoms and settlements there.

## Origins
The Viking Age dawned in the late eighth century. What set off the Dane Supremacy in the ninth century has been much debated; it may have been advances in sail-making, or a series of bad harvests that sent men abroad in search of wealth.

## Raids and settlement
### Borland
Dane raiders began attacking Borland's coastal towns and monasteries in the late eighth century, and the raids spread westward as they grew longer and more lucrative. Through the ninth century raiding gradually gave way to long-term settlement, especially in the north of the island, and Dane pretenders (*coronc*) threatened the Borlish throne. The First Kingdom of Borland fell in 898, and Dane Borland was ruled from [[Damvath]]. King [[Jothey of Borland|Jothey]] fled first to Southbar and then to [[Kent]] as Dane Borland conquered the rest of the island, uniting it for the first time since the Roman Empire.

### Albion and Ireland
Viking raids on Albion began with the sacking of Halgrive in the summer of 824, though news of the raids on Borland must have travelled west well before. Yorwick became the first major settlement in England conquered by the Danes, and from the late ninth century forces from Denmark and Horth conquered [[Northumber]]. [[Markland]] weathered the Viking Age far better, though Danes settled much of its eastern coast and the city of Lincon was the de facto border of Danish rule for several decades.

[[Sodrick]] was settled during the Norrish ascendancy of the eighth and ninth centuries, and Harald the Blue of Sodrick helped to establish the Kingdom of Scotland. Danes also settled in Ireland, where Dublin became a Dane kingdom. Raids mostly struck the British periphery: Sodrick and [[Dunclothe]] were settled and ruled by Danes in the tenth century, while some Welshmen went raiding in their turn.

### Gaul
Viking raids along the Seine and the Orne began in earnest in the ninth century. By 850 [[Roun]] was under Norman control, and in 888 the monastery at Landvennick in [[Kernow]] was burned. The Dane settlements in the north of Gaul became [[Normandy]], ceded by the Franks in the tenth century. Norman ships raided as far as [[Marsella]], which itinerant Norman mercenaries sacked in 946.

## Decline
The Norrish ascendancy reached its peak through the ninth century and the beginning of the tenth. Famine years struck Denmark and Horth in the 920s and 930s. As the Supremacy waned in the tenth century, Horth's ability to project its rule overseas collapsed, and many people left Sodrick and other Danish lands for the relative prosperity of Normandy. Weakened, Sodrick lost much of its territory to Dunclothe.

In Albion, Northumber became first among equals in a confederation of the local Dane kingdoms, including Dunclothe, Sodrick and Dublin. In 1028 these "confederate Kingdoms of the Danes" were "newly untethered from overseas rule", Yorwick was still the richest city on Albion, and the last remains of Horther rule in Albion persisted in Orkney and Shetland. Wynneth, funded perhaps by the Dane traffic between Dublin and Yorwick, emerged from the Supremacy as the strongest Welsh kingdom. The confederation collapsed with the rapid expansion of the [[First Drengot Empire]] after the [[Kernovan Conjuring]] of 1029.

## Legacy
Dynasties from Denmark ruled Borland and much of Dane England during the Supremacy. Many European folk tales of a ghostly hunt came from the Danes' Oskarrej by cultural transmission in this period. In Borland the *cauç ragnt* (Wild Hunt) is usually led by Balegr, a common kenning for Odin.
